# Ghana — Bank of Ghana (BoG)

## Regulatory Authority

Ghana's financial system is regulated by several institutions:

- **Bank of Ghana (BoG)** — the central bank, responsible for monetary policy, currency management, and the supervision of banks, savings and loans companies, finance houses, rural and community banks, microfinance institutions, and payment service providers. BoG is the primary AML/CFT supervisor for all deposit-taking institutions and electronic money issuers.
- **Securities and Exchange Commission Ghana (SEC Ghana)** — regulates capital markets, collective investment schemes, and securities dealers. Issues its own AML/CFT directives for the securities sector.
- **National Insurance Commission (NIC)** — regulates the insurance industry, including AML/CFT obligations for insurance intermediaries and life insurers.
- **Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC)** — Ghana's Financial Intelligence Unit, established under the Anti-Money Laundering Act. The FIC receives and analyses STRs and CTRs, disseminates financial intelligence to law enforcement, and coordinates Ghana's international AML/CFT cooperation.

---

## Key Regulations

- **Anti-Money Laundering Act 2020 (Act 1044)** — Ghana's primary AML statute, replacing the earlier AML Act 2008. Act 1044 significantly expanded the list of covered entities (now including real estate agents, car dealers, and accountants), strengthened beneficial ownership requirements, and aligned Ghana more closely with FATF 40 Recommendations.
- **Payment Systems and Services Act 2019 (Act 987)** — governs the licensing and operation of payment service providers, electronic money issuers, and payment system operators. This is the legal basis for mobile money licensing and regulation.
- **Bank of Ghana Act (Act 612, as amended)** — foundational statute establishing BoG's mandate, governance, and supervisory powers.
- **Banking Act (as amended, incorporating Banks and Specialised Deposit-Taking Institutions Act 2016, Act 930)** — prudential framework for all deposit-taking institutions.
- **Borrowers and Lenders Act 2020 (Act 1052)** — governs credit reporting, collateral security, and lending practices; relevant for understanding credit risk and data management.
- **Foreign Exchange Act 2006 (Act 723)** — governs foreign currency transactions, cross-border payments, and forex dealing; relevant for trade-based AML monitoring.

## AML/CFT Framework

The **Financial Intelligence Centre (FIC)** is Ghana's FIU, established under the Anti-Money Laundering Act. Key obligations and thresholds:

- **STR (Suspicious Transaction Report):** Mandatory for all accountable institutions upon formation of suspicion of money laundering, terrorist financing, or proliferation financing. Tipping-off prohibited. Reports submitted directly to the FIC.
- **CTR (Currency Transaction Report):** Required for cash transactions equal to or exceeding **GHS 10,000** (approximately USD 700 at current rates — note the Ghanaian Cedi has experienced significant depreciation, so compliance teams should verify current thresholds in BoG circulars).
- **Customer Due Diligence:** Risk-based framework requiring standard CDD for all new customer relationships and enhanced due diligence for higher-risk customers, including PEPs, non-residents, and customers from high-risk jurisdictions.
- **Beneficial Ownership:** Act 1044 requires identification of beneficial owners for legal entities. Ghana has established a Beneficial Ownership Registry.

**GIABA Membership:** Ghana is a member of GIABA (Inter-Governmental Action Group against Money Laundering in West Africa), the FATF-Style Regional Body (FSRB) for West Africa. GIABA conducts mutual evaluations of member countries. Ghana's mutual evaluation results provide context for understanding areas of ongoing AML/CFT development.

**Enforcement:** BoG has taken enforcement action against banks for AML/CFT failures, particularly following the 2019 banking sector cleanup that resulted in the revocation of licences of nine insolvent banks and financial institutions.

## Digital Finance and Mobile Money

Ghana is a leading mobile money market in Africa:

- **MTN Mobile Money (MTN MoMo)** — the dominant mobile money provider with over 20 million registered wallets, leveraging Ghana's largest mobile network. A significant portion of the country's informal economy transacts through MoMo.
- **Vodafone Cash** — second major mobile money provider, operated by Vodafone Ghana.
- **AirtelTigo Money** — third player following the merger of Airtel and Tigo operations.
- **GhIPSS (Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems)** — the national payment infrastructure company owned by BoG and the banking sector. GhIPSS provides mobile money interoperability, enabling transfers between different mobile money providers and between mobile money and bank accounts — a critical feature for financial inclusion.
- **Universal QR Code** — GhIPSS-managed QR payment system allowing merchants to accept payments from any mobile money or bank wallet through a single code.
- **GhanaPay** — bank-led mobile money wallet operated by the Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems on behalf of the banking sector, positioned as the banks' response to telco-led mobile money.
- **BoG Fintech Regulatory Sandbox** — launched to enable fintech innovation within a controlled regulatory environment. BoG has also issued an API framework and an Open Banking Policy to facilitate third-party financial services.

## Microfinance and Rural Banking

Ghana has a large and historically troubled microfinance sector:

- **Rural and Community Banks (RCBs)** — over 140 rural banks provide services in areas underserved by universal banks. Supervised by BoG through the ARB Apex Bank.
- **Savings and Loans Companies / Finance Houses** — mid-tier deposit-taking institutions that operated largely in urban areas.
- **2019 Sector Cleanup** — BoG revoked licences of over 400 microfinance companies, 23 savings and loans companies, and 8 finance houses following a systemic failure driven by mismanagement, fraud, and regulatory arbitrage. The cleanup resulted in a GHS 21 billion government bailout and significantly reshaped the microfinance landscape. This history is critical context for understanding Ghana's current AML/CFT supervisory posture.

## Key Terminology

- **Ghana Card** — the national biometric identity card issued by the National Identification Authority (NIA). Following reforms, the Ghana Card is now the primary KYC document required for opening bank accounts and registering mobile money wallets. Its widespread adoption has significantly improved identity verification capability.
- **GIABA** (Inter-Governmental Action Group against Money Laundering in West Africa) — the FATF-style regional body for West Africa (16 ECOWAS member states). Conducts mutual evaluations, provides technical assistance, and represents West Africa in FATF plenary processes.
- **Susu** — a traditional rotating savings and credit association (ROSCA) prevalent across Ghana and West Africa. Members contribute fixed amounts periodically; the pooled sum is paid to one member in rotation. Susu collectors operate formally and informally. Susu poses AML risks due to cash-intensive operations and limited documentation — BoG has issued guidance on formalising Susu activities.
- **GHS** — the Ghanaian Cedi, Ghana's currency. AML thresholds and financial data should always be verified against current exchange rates given historic volatility.
