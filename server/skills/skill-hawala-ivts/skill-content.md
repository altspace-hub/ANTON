# Domain Skill: Hawala & Informal Value Transfer Systems (IVTS)

You possess deep operational and regulatory expertise on hawala and all related informal value transfer systems. Apply the following reference material throughout your analysis whenever hawala, IVTS, informal remittances, or related typologies are relevant.

---

## 1. How Hawala Works — Core Mechanics

Hawala is a trust-based, broker-mediated value transfer system in which money moves without physically crossing borders at the time of transfer. The mechanism operates as follows:

**Step-by-step transaction flow:**
1. Customer A in Country X wants to send value to Recipient B in Country Y.
2. Customer A gives cash (or equivalent) to Hawaladar 1 (the local broker) in Country X, along with Recipient B's contact details and an agreed code word or PIN.
3. Hawaladar 1 contacts Hawaladar 2 in Country Y — typically by phone, messaging app, or encrypted channel — and instructs payment of the equivalent amount to Recipient B upon presentation of the code.
4. Hawaladar 2 pays Recipient B from their own float. No money has physically moved between countries.
5. Hawaladars 1 and 2 now have a debt between them. Settlement of inter-broker balances happens later and separately, through any of several mechanisms (see below).

**Key operational features:**
- Speed: transfers complete within hours, often faster than SWIFT.
- Cost: typically 1–3% commission, often lower than formal remittances.
- Reach: operates in geographies with no functioning banking infrastructure.
- Anonymity: minimal documentation, identity-checking left to brokers' discretion.
- Trust: the system is entirely trust-based — hawaladars rely on reputation and long-standing relationships.

**Inter-broker settlement methods:**
- Physical cash movement via couriers (lowest volume, high risk of detection).
- Trade-based settlement: over- or under-invoicing of goods to cancel the debt through a commercial transaction.
- Gold, diamonds, or commodities physically transferred across borders.
- Real estate transactions in one or both jurisdictions.
- Netting: Hawaladar 1 owes Hawaladar 2 in the opposite direction from another transaction, so both debts cancel.
- Digital assets: increasingly, hawaladars use cryptocurrency to settle inter-broker balances rapidly with pseudonymity.

The critical AML insight is that the settlement leg — not the customer-facing transfer — is where money actually moves, and it is often disguised within a trade or commercial transaction.

## 2. Legitimate Uses vs. Illicit Exploitation

**Legitimate uses — understand before flagging:**
- Diaspora remittances to underbanked home regions (South Asia, East Africa, Afghanistan, Somalia).
- Emergency transfers to conflict zones where banks are non-functional (Gaza, Yemen, Sudan, South Sudan).
- Cultural preference: hawala pre-dates modern banking by centuries. For many communities it is the default mechanism, not a suspicious choice.
- Low-cost alternative for workers sending small amounts to families (especially where formal bank fees are prohibitive).
- Last resort: in countries with capital controls (e.g., Iran, Venezuela), hawala may be the only way to move value.

**Illicit exploitation of hawala:**
- Moving proceeds of crime out of the jurisdiction in which they were generated, with no banking footprint.
- Sanctions evasion: circumventing asset freezes by routing value through unregulated brokers in multiple jurisdictions.
- Terrorist financing: small, rapid transfers to conflict zones. Hawala was used extensively for TF to Al-Qaeda, Taliban, and IS-affiliated networks, as documented in multiple FATF typologies reports.
- Tax evasion: transfers undeclared, no paper trail.
- Drug and wildlife trafficking proceeds routed through hawala networks.

The risk is anonymity and lack of audit trail — not the mechanism itself. A compliance officer must assess the customer's profile, corridor, and purpose holistically, not flag all hawala-associated activity automatically.

## 3. FATF Framework — Recommendation 14 (Formerly SR VI)

**FATF Special Recommendation VI (pre-2012)** addressed money or value transfer services (MVTS). This is now embedded in **FATF Recommendation 14** in the revised 2012 framework.

**Recommendation 14 requires countries to:**
- Ensure all MVTS providers — including hawaladars — are licensed or registered.
- Subject MVTS providers to the full AML/CFT obligation regime applicable to financial institutions (CDD, record-keeping, STR filing, sanctions screening).
- Maintain a register of licensed/registered MVTS providers.
- Sanction or criminalise unlicensed/unregistered operation.
- Apply Recommendation 16 (the Travel Rule) to MVTS transactions — transmit originator and beneficiary information.

**FATF Guidance on IVTS (2013):** The definitive guidance document. It distinguishes between:
- **Formal MVTS:** Licensed or registered with a regulator.
- **Informal MVTS (IVTS):** Operating outside regulatory supervision — the primary concern.

**FATF typologies on hawala misuse:** Multiple FATF typologies reports document hawala abuse patterns:
- "Money Laundering Using New Payment Methods" (2010)
- "Professional Money Laundering" (2018)
- "Trade-Based Money Laundering" (2020) — cross-references hawala and TBML overlap.
- FATF IVTS case studies: documented cases from Pakistan, UAE, Somalia, UK, and US.

**Recommendation 16 (Travel Rule) applied to MVTS:** Hawaladars must collect and transmit originator name, account/identifier, address or national ID number, and beneficiary name and account/identifier. This is the single most challenging obligation for hawala operators in practice.

## 4. Red Flags for Hawala Activity

The following indicators, assessed in combination and in context, may suggest hawala activity. No single indicator is determinative.

**Customer behaviour:**
- Regular transfers to high-risk jurisdictions (Afghanistan, Pakistan, Somalia, Iran, Yemen) with no plausible economic or family rationale.
- Use of multiple currencies in a single relationship without commercial explanation.
- Customer refers to a third party who will "collect" funds but provides no verifiable relationship.
- Reluctance to provide purpose of transfer; vague or inconsistent explanations ("helping family").
- Transfers structured just below reporting thresholds (structuring) over multiple days or accounts.
- Customer has no apparent income source consistent with transfer volumes.

**Transaction patterns:**
- Frequent round-number transactions (e.g., exactly $500, $1,000, $2,000) — suggests use of standardised code amounts.
- Funds received and immediately withdrawn in cash, with no intervening spending pattern.
- Multiple unrelated individuals sending to the same recipient in Country Y (consistent with a hawaladar aggregating client funds).
- Account used as a pass-through: large inflows immediately offset by outflows, near-zero closing balance.
- Transactions via money service businesses (MSBs) in jurisdictions with weak AML oversight.

**Business account indicators:**
- Business account receives transfers from private individuals unrelated to the business's sector.
- MSB or travel agency account shows transaction volumes inconsistent with declared business size.
- Account used by a known or suspected licensed hawala provider shows patterns consistent with IVTS brokerage.

## 5. Major Hawala Corridors

**South Asia ↔ Gulf (dominant global corridor):**
- India, Pakistan, Bangladesh diaspora workers in UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain.
- Estimated ~$100 billion+ in annual remittance flows to South Asia, with a significant informal share.
- Dubai and Sharjah are major hawala nodes — the UAE's CBUAE operates a Registered Hawala Provider (RHP) programme to formalise operators.

**East Africa ↔ Middle East:**
- Somali-Kenyan community using xawilaad (Somali term for hawala) extensively.
- Somalia: formal banking collapsed in 1991. Hawala providers (Dahabshiil, Taaj, Amal Express) functioned as the country's de facto financial system for decades. FATF-designated High Risk Jurisdiction.
- Kenya ↔ Somalia: major corridor; Kenyan bank de-risking of Somali MSBs has created compliance gaps.

**South Asia ↔ UK / Europe:**
- Pakistani and Bangladeshi diaspora in the UK use traditional hawala channels. Significant HMRC and NCA enforcement activity in this corridor.

**Afghanistan:**
- Hawala IS the primary financial system — an estimated 80–90% of all financial transactions use hawala.
- Post-2021 Taliban takeover, formal banking has largely collapsed. International humanitarian organisations must use hawala networks to move funds inside the country.
- FATF-designated High Risk Jurisdiction. Afghan hawaladars operate extensively in Pakistan, UAE, and Europe.

**West Africa:**
- "Njangi" system in Cameroon (community rotating savings and credit associations with IVTS characteristics).
- Nigeria, Ghana: mobile money and informal transfer systems converge; large diaspora in the UK, US, Europe.

## 6. Investigation Techniques

**Following the settlement trail:**
- The customer-facing leg of a hawala transaction may be invisible to investigators. Focus on the settlement mechanism: identify unusual trade flows, commodity exports, bulk cash movements, or real estate transactions between the two jurisdictions consistent with settlement.
- Mirror transaction analysis: if Hawaladar A in Country X is settling a net debt to Hawaladar B in Country Y, look for compensating flows — goods exported at inflated prices, or bulk cash reported at borders.

**Network link analysis:**
- Build a network graph of all counterparties to a suspected hawaladar's account. Hawaladars typically have a small number of regular counter-broker relationships transacted at regular intervals. Map these to identify the network structure.
- Cross-reference against licensed MVTS registries to identify unlicensed operators embedded in the network.

**Interview techniques for hawala investigations:**
- Approach with cultural sensitivity — hawala operators may be cooperative but not volunteer compliance information.
- Verify records: hawaladars maintain ledgers (often handwritten, in Urdu, Arabic, or Pashto). Demand production of the hawala book.
- Ask specifically about settlement: how are inter-broker balances resolved, with whom, and through what mechanism?

**Coordination with FIUs in destination countries:**
- Egmont Group cooperation essential — send financial intelligence packages to the FIU in the destination corridor.
- MENAFATF (Middle East and North Africa FATF-Style Regional Body) for Gulf-South Asia corridor.
- ESAAMLG for East Africa.

**Travel record correlation:**
- Hawala cash couriers travel with physical cash. Correlate suspected hawaladar travel records (passport stamps, airline bookings) with transaction dates and volumes. Cash declarations at borders are also an investigative resource.

## 7. Regulatory Approaches by Jurisdiction

| Jurisdiction | Regulatory Requirement | Regulator | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | Registration as Money Service Business mandatory | HMRC (registration); FCA (authorisation for larger operators) | Criminal offence to operate unregistered. NCA and HMRC joint enforcement. Hawaladars must file SARs. |
| United States | FinCEN registration as Money Services Business | FinCEN (registration); State money transmitter licences also required | Bank Secrecy Act applies in full. Currency Transaction Reports for $10,000+. Significant prosecution record for unlicensed hawala (18 USC 1960). |
| United Arab Emirates | Registered Hawala Provider (RHP) programme | Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) | UAE has the most developed hawala registration system globally. ~200+ registered providers. Annual registration renewal. Full AML/CFT obligations. CBUAE conducts onsite inspections. |
| Pakistan | Pakistan Remittance Initiative (PRI) | State Bank of Pakistan | Formalisation push — incentivising diaspora to use formal channels (Roshan Digital Account). Unlicensed hawala criminally sanctioned but enforcement uneven. |
| India | Hawala is illegal (defined as "illegal remittance" under FEMA 1999) | Reserve Bank of India | Only RBI-authorised dealers may conduct cross-border remittances. ED (Enforcement Directorate) actively prosecutes hawala operators. Despite illegality, significant volumes persist. |
| Saudi Arabia | SAMA licensing for money changers and exchange houses | Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) | Strict enforcement; unlicensed hawala subject to criminal prosecution. |
| Australia | Registration with AUSTRAC as a remittance network provider | AUSTRAC | Cascade registration model — network providers register sub-agents. Significant enforcement history (e.g., Western Union, smaller operators). |

## 8. Distinctions from Related IVTS Concepts

| System | Region | Local Name | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hawala | South Asia, Middle East, Africa | Hawala (Arabic/Urdu) | Trust-based, debt assignment, broker network |
| Fei-ch'ien | China, Chinese diaspora | "Flying money" | Historically identical mechanism; modern equivalent in underground banking in Chinese communities |
| Hundi | South Asia (historical and current) | Hundi (Hindi/Urdu) | Precursor to hawala; negotiable instrument-based; now often used interchangeably with hawala |
| Padala | Philippines | Padala ("to send") | Informal remittance via trusted couriers; overlaps with hawala mechanics in Philippine diaspora communities |
| Xawilaad | Somalia | Xawilaad | Somali hawala; critical national infrastructure post-banking collapse |
| Fei | West Africa | Various local terms | Hawala-equivalent systems in Francophone West Africa; often embedded in market trader networks |
| Pochteca | Latin America | Historical | Pre-Columbian value transfer; modern informal systems in Latin America differ but share some characteristics |

All of these share the defining IVTS characteristic: value moves between locations without a corresponding physical movement of currency at the time of transfer. The settlement — which may be delayed, netted, or disguised — is the AML risk point.
