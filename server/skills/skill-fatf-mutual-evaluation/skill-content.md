# Methodology Skill: FATF Mutual Evaluation Methodology

You possess authoritative expertise in the FATF mutual evaluation process, the 40 Recommendations, the 11 Immediate Outcomes, and the strategic and operational implications of evaluations for both governments and private sector institutions. Apply this framework precisely and accurately whenever FATF assessments, country compliance ratings, grey-listing, or AML/CFT system design are relevant.

---

## 1. FATF — Institutional Overview

The **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** is an intergovernmental policy-making body established in 1989 by the G7 Paris Summit. Its mandate is to set international standards for combating money laundering, terrorist financing, and proliferation financing (ML/TF/PF).

**Membership (as of 2025):**
- 39 member jurisdictions + 2 regional organisations (European Commission, Gulf Co-operation Council).
- Members include: US, UK, EU member states, China, Japan, India, Australia, Canada, South Africa, Brazil, Argentina, Saudi Arabia, UAE, and others.
- Headquartered in Paris, OECD premises.

**FATF-Style Regional Bodies (FSRBs):**
FATF extends its reach globally through a network of regional bodies that conduct mutual evaluations of non-FATF-member countries and provide technical assistance:
- MONEYVAL (Council of Europe)
- GIABA (Inter-Governmental Action Group Against Money Laundering in West Africa)
- GAFILAT (Latin America)
- APG (Asia-Pacific Group on Money Laundering)
- ESAAMLG (Eastern and Southern Africa)
- GABAC (Central Africa)
- MENAFATF (Middle East and North Africa)
- EAG (Eurasian Group)
- CFATF (Caribbean)

**FATF 40 Recommendations:** The global AML/CFT/CPF standard. First issued in 1990, revised significantly in 2001 (to add TF), revised comprehensively in 2012, and updated continuously through interpretive notes and guidance papers. The 2012 revision introduced the current Methodology and the Immediate Outcomes framework.

## 2. The Two-Pillar Assessment Framework

FATF's current methodology (adopted 2012, applied from the 4th Round of Evaluations) assesses countries on two separate but linked dimensions:

### Pillar 1: Technical Compliance (TC)

Technical compliance measures whether a country has the **legal and institutional framework** required by the 40 Recommendations. It answers the question: **Are the right laws, regulations, and institutions in place?**

**Rating scale:** Each Recommendation is rated:
- **C — Compliant:** All requirements are met with no more than minor shortcomings.
- **LC — Largely Compliant:** Only minor shortcomings.
- **PC — Partially Compliant:** Moderate shortcomings; some requirements are met.
- **NC — Non-Compliant:** Major shortcomings; most requirements not met.

### Pillar 2: Effectiveness (Immediate Outcomes)

Effectiveness measures whether the AML/CFT system **actually works** in practice. It answers the question: **Are outcomes being achieved?** A country may have technically compliant laws on paper but near-zero effectiveness if those laws are not enforced.

**Rating scale:** Each Immediate Outcome is rated:
- **HE — High Effectiveness:** Country demonstrates comprehensive and sustained actions; demonstrating the appropriate effectiveness level.
- **SE — Substantial Effectiveness:** Country demonstrates strong actions and substantial effectiveness.
- **ME — Moderate Effectiveness:** Country demonstrates some actions but has moderate effectiveness.
- **LE — Low Effectiveness:** Country demonstrates weak actions and low effectiveness.

**Critical distinction:** A country can have TC ratings of Largely Compliant across all 40 Recommendations yet achieve only Low Effectiveness across the Immediate Outcomes — a pattern common in developing countries where laws exist but enforcement capacity is absent. Conversely, effective informal enforcement may exist even where the technical legal framework has gaps.

## 3. The 11 Immediate Outcomes — Full Reference

Each Immediate Outcome has a defined "core issue" and a set of specific effectiveness characteristics that assessors examine. The 11 IOs collectively cover the entire AML/CFT/CPF system from risk understanding through to final confiscation.

| IO | Title | Core Issue | Key Metrics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IO.1** | Risk, Policy and Coordination | Is the country's risk understood, and does it have coordinated policies? | National Risk Assessment quality; inter-agency coordination; National AML/CFT Strategy |
| **IO.2** | International Cooperation | Does the country cooperate effectively with foreign counterparts? | MLA requests sent/received; Egmont Group engagement; extradition record; spontaneous information exchange |
| **IO.3** | Supervision | Are FIs and DNFBPs supervised for AML/CFT? | Supervisor risk understanding; onsite inspection programme; sanctions applied; remediation effectiveness |
| **IO.4** | Preventive Measures | Do FIs and DNFBPs apply AML/CFT measures? | Quality of CDD; STR quality and timeliness; internal controls; DNFBP compliance |
| **IO.5** | Legal Persons and Arrangements | Is beneficial ownership information adequate and accessible? | BO register quality; timeliness; law enforcement access; misuse prevention |
| **IO.6** | Financial Intelligence | Is financial intelligence effectively used? | STR quality and utility; FIU analytical output; law enforcement use of FIU products |
| **IO.7** | ML Investigation and Prosecution | Are ML offences effectively investigated and prosecuted? | ML case numbers; conviction rates; parallel financial investigation; case complexity |
| **IO.8** | Confiscation | Are proceeds of crime effectively confiscated? | Confiscation statistics; value confiscated; provisional measures; asset management |
| **IO.9** | TF Investigation and Prosecution | Is TF effectively investigated and prosecuted? | TF case numbers; consistency with risk; preventive disruptions |
| **IO.10** | TF Preventive Measures and Financial Sanctions | Are financial sanctions for TF implemented without delay? | Sanctions designation implementation; NPO monitoring; FI awareness of TF risks |
| **IO.11** | PF Financial Sanctions | Are proliferation financing sanctions implemented without delay? | UN Security Council Resolutions implementation (1718/1737 regimes); FI screening effectiveness |

## 4. The Mutual Evaluation Process — Step by Step

**Timeline:** A complete mutual evaluation typically takes 18–24 months from launch to plenary adoption.

**Phase 1 — Self-Assessment (6–12 months before country visit):**
The country under review submits a detailed questionnaire covering all 40 Recommendations and 11 IOs. This includes: legislation, institutional structure, statistics (STRs, CTRs, ML prosecutions, confiscations, supervisory actions), and self-assessed ratings. Quality of the self-assessment directly impacts the evaluation — poorly prepared self-assessments impede assessors and may result in lower ratings.

**Phase 2 — Assessor Team Composition:**
FATF assigns a team of typically 5–7 assessors drawn from peer member jurisdictions. Assessors are legal, financial, and law enforcement experts. Each assessor covers specific Recommendations aligned to their expertise. The team is led by a Lead Assessor (typically an experienced FATF Secretariat member or senior peer expert).

**Phase 3 — Off-Site Review (3–4 months):**
Assessors review the self-assessment, legislation, and supporting materials. They develop questions and identify gaps. Pre-visit correspondence with the country to request clarifications and additional documentation.

**Phase 4 — Country Visit (typically 2 weeks):**
Intensive programme of meetings with:
- Government ministries (Finance, Justice, Interior, Foreign Affairs)
- Central bank / financial regulator(s)
- FIU
- Law enforcement (police, customs, prosecutors, asset recovery)
- Supervisory bodies (for FIs and DNFBPs)
- Private sector (banks, insurance, legal/accounting, real estate, casinos — selected to represent market coverage)
- Civil society (where appropriate)

Assessors test not just what institutions say they do, but what the data and case studies demonstrate. They will ask for specific ML/TF case files, supervisor inspection reports, and FIU statistical outputs.

**Phase 5 — Draft Report:**
Assessors produce a draft Mutual Evaluation Report (MER). Drafted by the assessment team, reviewed by FATF Secretariat. Typically 200–400 pages. Contains rated findings for all 40 TCs and 11 IOs, plus recommended actions.

**Phase 6 — Country Review:**
The assessed country receives the draft and can challenge factual errors or provide additional evidence. This is NOT an opportunity to re-negotiate ratings — only factual corrections are accepted at this stage.

**Phase 7 — FATF Plenary Adoption:**
The report is presented to a FATF Plenary (held three times per year). Plenary members review and adopt the report. The assessed country presents its position. Plenary may ask clarifying questions. Rating decisions are final upon adoption.

**Phase 8 — Publication and Follow-Up:**
MER published on the FATF website. Country placed in follow-up category:
- **Regular Follow-Up:** Sufficient progress; biennial progress reports to FATF Plenary.
- **Enhanced Follow-Up:** Significant deficiencies; annual progress reports; more intensive scrutiny.
- **Increased Monitoring (Grey List):** Formal listing; agreed action plan with deadlines; high-level political commitment required.
- **Call for Action (Black List):** Extreme deficiencies; call on all FATF members to apply countermeasures.

## 5. The FATF Grey List — Increased Monitoring

**Grey listing is a formal FATF process, not an informal label.** The process:
1. During or after mutual evaluation, FATF identifies strategic AML/CFT/CPF deficiencies that require urgent attention.
2. Country must provide high-level political commitment to address deficiencies within a defined timeframe.
3. Country formally listed on the "Jurisdictions Under Increased Monitoring" list, published on FATF's website.
4. Country agrees to a specific, time-bound Action Plan with measurable items.
5. FATF monitors progress at each Plenary. If sufficient progress is made within agreed timeframes, the country is de-listed.
6. If insufficient progress, the country may be escalated to the "Call for Action" (Black List).

**Consequences of grey listing for the financial sector:**
- Correspondent banks apply enhanced due diligence to all transactions involving the grey-listed country — significantly increasing correspondent banking costs and sometimes triggering de-risking (withdrawal of correspondent relationships).
- IMF and World Bank may reference the listing in Article IV consultations and project assessments.
- Reputational damage impacts foreign direct investment.
- Insurance and trade finance costs may increase.
- Regulatory expectation: financial institutions in non-listed countries must apply EDD to customers and transactions involving grey-listed jurisdictions (per FATF Recommendation 19).

**Recent grey list examples (relevant as of 2025):**
- **UAE:** Listed February 2022; de-listed February 2024 after addressing deficiencies in beneficial ownership, DNFBP supervision, and TF prosecution.
- **South Africa:** Listed June 2023; de-listed October 2023 — one of the fastest de-listings on record, reflecting rapid legislative action.
- **Nigeria:** Listed February 2023; remains under enhanced follow-up; key deficiencies in DNFBP supervision, BO register, and financial sanctions implementation.
- **Cayman Islands:** Listed February 2021; de-listed October 2024.
- **Philippines:** Listed June 2021; de-listed June 2023.

## 6. Preparation Strategy for Countries

Countries undergoing mutual evaluation benefit from structured preparation. The following areas are consistently the most challenging and should receive priority focus:

**Effectiveness evidence gathering (18+ months pre-visit):**
The single most important preparation step is building a verifiable track record of outcomes. Assessors look at statistics for the last 3–5 years. Jurisdictions with limited conviction records, low confiscation values, and weak STR utilisation consistently receive Low or Moderate effectiveness ratings regardless of legal framework quality. Actions: increase ML prosecutions, pursue complex cases, document confiscation statistics systematically.

**DNFBP supervision (most common weakness globally):**
Real estate agents, lawyers, accountants, notaries, trust and company service providers, and dealers in precious metals and stones are typically under-supervised relative to banks. Assessors expect a functioning supervisory programme: risk assessment of the DNFBP sector, supervisory inspections, sanctions for non-compliance, demonstrated understanding by DNFBPs of their AML/CFT obligations.

**Beneficial ownership registers:**
IO.5 is consistently rated ME or LE in developing countries. Registers must be: (a) comprehensive, (b) accurate (verified, not self-declared), (c) accessible to competent authorities in a timely manner, and (d) updated within defined timeframes on material changes. Sanctions for non-disclosure must be applied in practice.

**STR quality:**
Many jurisdictions produce large numbers of STRs but of poor quality (defensive filing, template-based, no analytical value). Assessors examine whether FIU analytical products based on STRs are used by law enforcement. FIU-law enforcement feedback loops must be demonstrably functional.

**National Risk Assessment (NRA):**
IO.1 requires a credible, up-to-date NRA that informs the allocation of supervisory and law enforcement resources. The NRA must identify the country's principal ML/TF threats and vulnerabilities, not simply endorse the existing supervisory structure.

**Private sector engagement:**
Assessors interview private sector participants during the country visit. Banks, insurers, and DNFBPs must demonstrate they understand their ML/TF risk exposure and have implemented proportionate controls. Assessors form impressions during private sector sessions — a bank's compliance officer who cannot articulate their institution's ML risk rating for a specific customer segment will reflect poorly on the supervisory outcome.

## 7. Implications for Private Sector Institutions

**Risk rating of jurisdictions:** Institutions must factor FATF status into country risk ratings. A grey-listed jurisdiction requires EDD for all customers and transactions. A Recommendation 19 disclosure must appear in the institution's risk appetite statement and CDD procedures.

**Correspondent banking:** Banks correspondent to institutions in grey-listed countries must apply enhanced scrutiny. Many international correspondent banks reduce or withdraw relationships to minimise regulatory risk — this is the "de-risking" problem that grey listing directly causes.

**DNFBP compliance:** Real estate agents, lawyers, and accountants preparing for domestic regulatory scrutiny ahead of a mutual evaluation should expect supervisory inspections, AML questionnaires, and potential enforcement actions if deficiencies are found.

**Post-evaluation regulatory uplift:** Jurisdictions exiting grey listing (or achieving only ME/LE ratings without being listed) typically face a wave of new regulations implementing action plan commitments. Private sector institutions should monitor legislative pipelines following a mutual evaluation publication.
