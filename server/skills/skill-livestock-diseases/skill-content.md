# Thematic Skill: Livestock Health -- Common Diseases

This skill gives you comprehensive, practical knowledge of major livestock and poultry diseases affecting smallholders across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. Use it to help farmers, extension workers, and veterinary staff identify diseases by their symptoms, respond appropriately, understand vaccination needs, and know when to call a veterinarian. All information is written in plain, practical language. Where medical or veterinary terms are unavoidable, they are explained immediately.

---

## 1. CATTLE DISEASES

### East Coast Fever (ECF)

Caused by Theileria parva, a microscopic parasite transmitted by the brown ear tick (Rhipicephalus appendiculatus). Present across East, Southern, and Central Africa.

Symptoms: High fever (40 degrees Celsius and above), swollen lymph nodes (especially the parotid node behind the ear -- this is a key diagnostic sign), laboured breathing, nasal discharge, weight loss, and death within 2 to 4 weeks if untreated.

First response: Isolate the animal from the herd immediately. Call a veterinarian as soon as possible. Treatment is expensive but effective -- Buparvaquone (Butalex) injection given by a vet. Without treatment, mortality is very high. Tick control (regular acaricide dipping or spraying) is essential to prevent infection.

Vaccination: Live immunisation using the Infection and Treatment Method (ITM) is available in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. It is expensive but provides lasting protection. Discuss with your vet or district livestock officer.

Prevention: Regular acaricide dipping or spraying on schedule (weekly in high-tick areas). Inspect ears and skin folds for ticks. Keep pastures short.

---

### Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD)

A highly contagious viral disease affecting all cloven-hooved animals (cattle, sheep, goats, pigs). Can spread very rapidly between farms through direct contact, contaminated clothing or equipment, and even air over short distances.

Symptoms: Blisters (fluid-filled sores) on the feet (between the toes and on the coronary band), blisters inside the mouth and on the tongue, excessive salivation (drooling), lameness, weight loss, and dramatic reduction in milk production. Rarely fatal in adult animals but kills young calves. Animals recover but may have lasting foot damage.

IMPORTANT: REPORT TO AUTHORITIES IMMEDIATELY. FMD is a notifiable disease in most countries. Report to the nearest livestock officer, veterinary office, or government authority. Do not move animals from the farm. Quarantine the entire farm. Disinfect footwear and equipment with a 1% solution of sodium carbonate (washing soda).

Vaccination: Vaccines are available and effective. Vaccinate twice per year. The vaccine must match the circulating strain in your region -- your district veterinary office will advise which product to use.

Economic impact: FMD causes export bans, trade loss, and reduced productivity even when animals survive. It is one of the most economically damaging livestock diseases globally.

---

### Lumpy Skin Disease (LSD)

A viral disease of cattle transmitted by blood-sucking insects (mosquitoes, stable flies, biting midges). Spreading in East Africa and now present in Europe and Asia.

Symptoms: Fever, followed by firm nodular skin lesions (lumps) all over the body -- especially on the head, neck, udder, and legs. Lesions may ulcerate. Reduced milk production, weight loss, infertility, and lameness due to skin lesions on legs.

First response: Quarantine affected animal. Call a veterinarian. Supportive treatment (anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics to prevent secondary infection). Insect control to reduce spread.

Vaccination: Available and effective. Annual vaccination recommended in affected areas.

---

### Brucellosis

A bacterial disease caused by Brucella abortus in cattle. ZOONOTIC -- it can spread to humans. Humans contract undulant fever (recurring fever, sweating, joint pain, fatigue) from handling infected animals, placentas, or aborted foetuses, or from drinking raw milk.

Symptoms in cattle: Abortion in the last trimester of pregnancy (the most obvious sign), retained placenta, reduced fertility, reduced milk production. Bulls may develop orchitis (swollen testicles).

IMPORTANT: Brucellosis is a notifiable disease in most countries. Report suspected cases. Do not handle aborted material without gloves and protective clothing. Do not allow dogs or children near aborted material. Do not drink raw milk.

First response: Call a veterinarian to test the animal. In many national programmes, test-and-cull is the policy (positive animals are slaughtered and compensated). Do not sell positive animals.

Vaccination: Female calves aged 3 to 8 months are vaccinated once with S19 or RB51 vaccine in national programmes. Do not vaccinate pregnant or adult females without veterinary guidance.

---

### Blackleg (Black Quarter)

A clostridial disease caused by Clostridium chauvoei, primarily affecting young cattle aged 6 months to 2 years. The spores persist in the soil for many years.

Symptoms: Sudden death is common. In less acute cases: sudden severe lameness, the affected leg (usually a hind leg or shoulder) becomes swollen and hot, then cold and dark. When you press the skin you can feel a crackling sensation (gas under the skin). The animal is in extreme pain, stops eating, has high fever.

First response: Treat affected animals with high doses of penicillin immediately -- but by the time you see symptoms, it is often too late. Vaccinate all remaining healthy animals in the herd immediately with multi-clostridial vaccine to prevent spread. Call a veterinarian.

Vaccination: Annual vaccination with a multi-clostridial vaccine (covers Blackleg plus other clostridial diseases) at weaning or before high-risk season. In high-risk areas, vaccinate calves at 3 months old and again at weaning.

---

### Bovine Respiratory Disease (BRD / Shipping Fever)

A complex of viral and bacterial infections that commonly affects cattle under stress -- especially when transported, mixed with new animals, or moved from pasture to feedlot.

Symptoms: Cough, nasal discharge (clear to thick yellow-green), fever (39.5 to 41.5 degrees Celsius), rapid breathing, reduced appetite, dull eyes, depression, standing apart from the herd.

First response: Isolate the affected animal. Antibiotic treatment (prescribed by a vet -- commonly oxytetracycline, tulathromycin, or florfenicol). Anti-inflammatory drugs reduce fever and inflammation. Keep the animal in a comfortable, low-stress environment. Good hydration is important.

Prevention: Reduce stress at transport, mixing, and weaning. Ensure good ventilation in housing. Avoid overcrowding. Vaccinate against key viral causes before stressful events.

---

### Trypanosomiasis (Sleeping Sickness in Cattle)

A parasitic disease caused by Trypanosoma species, transmitted by tsetse flies. A serious cause of cattle mortality and production loss in sub-Saharan Africa (tsetse belt).

Symptoms: Progressive weight loss despite eating, rough coat, anaemia (pale inner eyelids), swollen lymph nodes, weakness, fever, and eventual death if untreated.

First response: Treatment with Diminazene aceturate (Berenil) or Isometamidium chloride (Samorin/Trypamidium). These drugs are effective if given early. Tsetse fly control (traps, insecticide-treated targets, insecticide-treated cattle). Call a veterinarian to confirm diagnosis.

Prevention: In West Africa, use trypanotolerant breeds such as N'Dama, Muturu, or Shorthorn cattle that survive with lower levels of trypanosomes. Avoid moving cattle through high-tsetse areas at peak biting times.

### Tick Control Overview

Ticks transmit multiple serious diseases (ECF, Anaplasmosis, Babesiosis, Heartwater, and more). Tick control is fundamental to cattle health in sub-Saharan Africa.

- Regular dipping or spraying with acaricide (chemical tick-killer) on schedule -- weekly in high-risk seasons, fortnightly in lower seasons.
- Resistance to acaricides is developing. Rotate between chemical classes (organophosphates, synthetic pyrethroids, amidines) to delay resistance. Ask your vet what is working in your area.
- Inspect animals regularly, especially ears, groin, and tail base.
- Do not overdip -- it wastes chemical and increases resistance development.

## 2. GOAT AND SHEEP DISEASES

### Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR)

PPR (sometimes called goat plague) is a highly contagious viral disease of goats and sheep. It is one of the most serious diseases affecting smallholder goat and sheep farmers globally. The virus is related to rinderpest (a cattle disease that has been eradicated).

Symptoms: Sudden high fever (over 40 degrees Celsius), watery discharge from eyes and nose that becomes thick and crusting, sores and erosions in the mouth (animals salivate heavily), severe diarrhoea (often watery and foul-smelling), pneumonia (coughing, laboured breathing), rapid death. Mortality in unvaccinated flocks can reach 50 to 80%.

IMPORTANT: REPORT TO AUTHORITIES IMMEDIATELY. PPR is a notifiable disease. Quarantine the farm, do not move animals. Isolate sick animals from the rest of the flock.

Vaccination: PPR vaccine is available, highly effective, and relatively inexpensive. Annual vaccination is recommended. All animals in the flock should be vaccinated at the same time. The vaccine provides long-lasting protection.

---

### Goat Pox and Sheep Pox

Viral diseases causing pox lesions. Goat Pox and Sheep Pox are different viruses but similar in presentation.

Symptoms: Fever, followed by pox lesions (flat red spots that become raised and then form scabs) on the skin of the face, legs, and udder. Respiratory signs (cough, nasal discharge). In severe cases, lesions can be internal and cause death.

First response: Quarantine affected animals. Call a veterinarian. Supportive treatment (antibiotics for secondary infections, wound care). Rest and good nutrition.

Vaccination: Vaccines available. Annual vaccination recommended in endemic areas.

---

### Contagious Caprine Pleuropneumonia (CCPP)

A serious bacterial lung disease of goats (not sheep), caused by Mycoplasma capricolum. Highly contagious.

Symptoms: Sudden onset of respiratory distress -- coughing, laboured breathing, extended neck (trying to breathe), nasal discharge, fever, animals stand with elbows turned out. Death can occur within a few days in acute cases.

First response: Call a veterinarian immediately. Antibiotic treatment (oxytetracycline) is effective if started early. Quarantine affected animals.

Vaccination: Vaccines available in some countries. Check with your district veterinary office.

---

### Internal Parasites (Helminths / Worms) in Goats and Sheep

Internal parasites are the most important health problem affecting small ruminants (goats and sheep) globally. They are a major cause of poor growth, low productivity, and death, especially in young animals.

Key parasite: Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm) is the most dangerous. It is a blood-sucking worm that lives in the abomasum (stomach). Heavily infected animals become severely anaemic -- their inner eyelids turn pale (white) instead of pink. They may develop a bottle jaw (fluid accumulation under the jaw). Weight loss, weakness, and death follow.

FAMACHA scoring: FAMACHA is a practical field method to detect anaemia caused by Haemonchus. You look at the inner eyelid of each animal and compare its colour to a FAMACHA card showing 5 colour categories from red-pink (healthy) to white (severely anaemic). Only treat animals that score 3, 4, or 5 (pale). This targeted treatment reduces the amount of anthelmintic (deworming drug) used, which is critical because resistance to anthelmintics is a very serious and growing problem globally.

Anthelmintic resistance: Resistance to the common drug classes (benzimidazoles like albendazole, levamisole, macrocyclic lactones like ivermectin) is widespread. Best practices:
- Do NOT treat the whole flock routinely on a calendar basis.
- Use FAMACHA and body condition scoring to identify animals that need treatment.
- Do not underdose -- weigh animals and calculate the correct dose.
- Rotate drug classes only when you have evidence that the current class is failing.
- Quarantine and treat all new animals entering the flock before mixing.

Prevention: Good nutrition strengthens animal resistance. Avoid overgrazing (worm larvae concentrate at the base of short grass). Use browse (tree leaves and bushes) which has natural tannins that suppress worm burden. Alternate grazing between small ruminants and cattle (cattle do not host the same worm species).

### Orf (Contagious Ecthyma / Contagious Pustular Dermatitis)

A viral disease of sheep and goats, caused by a parapoxvirus. Also called scabby mouth. ZOONOTIC -- humans can get painful skin sores on hands from handling infected animals.

Symptoms: Crusty scabs around the mouth, lips, and nose, and sometimes on feet and udder. Animals are painful when eating. Lambs and kids may lose weight due to difficulty nursing.

First response: Supportive care. Remove thick crusts carefully (wear gloves), apply antiseptic or wound spray. Secondary bacterial infections are common -- treat with antibiotics if needed. The disease usually resolves in 1 to 4 weeks.

Vaccination: Live attenuated vaccine is available. It is applied to a small scarified area of skin. The animal develops a mild lesion at the vaccination site and becomes immune. Useful in areas with repeated outbreaks.

## 3. PIG DISEASES

### African Swine Fever (ASF)

ASF is one of the most dangerous livestock diseases in the world. It affects only pigs and wild boar. There is NO vaccine and NO treatment. Mortality in infected pigs is typically 100%. It is spreading rapidly across Africa, Asia, and Europe.

Symptoms: Sudden deaths with no prior warning. Or: high fever (above 40 degrees Celsius), loss of appetite, weakness, skin discolouration (bluish-purple, especially on ears, snout, and belly), haemorrhages (bleeding) visible on the skin, vomiting and diarrhoea (which may be bloody), difficulty breathing.

IMPORTANT: REPORT TO AUTHORITIES IMMEDIATELY. ASF is a notifiable disease. Stamping-out policy -- all pigs on the farm are typically killed and disposed of. Do not sell sick pigs or move any pigs from the farm. Do not slaughter and eat sick pigs -- this spreads the disease through food waste.

Prevention (biosecurity is the only defence):
- NEVER feed kitchen waste or swill (cooked or uncooked food scraps) to pigs -- this is the single most common route of spread globally.
- Prevent all contact between domestic pigs and wild boar.
- Do not allow visitors access to pig pens without disinfection of footwear.
- Clean and disinfect pig housing between batches.
- Buy pigs only from known disease-free sources.
- Keep records of any pig movements.

### Classical Swine Fever (CSF / Hog Cholera)

A viral disease similar in appearance to ASF but caused by a different virus. Unlike ASF, vaccines ARE available.

Symptoms: High fever, weakness, staggering, haemorrhages on the skin, constipation followed by diarrhoea, blue discolouration of extremities, death.

Vaccination: Vaccines are available. Vaccinate in endemic areas on schedule recommended by your vet.

## 4. POULTRY DISEASES

### Newcastle Disease (ND)

Newcastle Disease is the most important poultry disease for smallholders globally. It can cause near-total flock mortality in unvaccinated village chickens within 3 to 5 days. It affects chickens, ducks, geese, turkeys, and other birds.

Symptoms: Sudden deaths with no warning (sometimes the first sign is simply dead birds). Other signs: twisted neck or paralysis (birds lying on their backs or turning in circles -- a distinctive sign), drooping wings, green or watery diarrhoea, nasal and eye discharge, gasping and coughing, dramatic drop in egg production, soft-shelled or abnormal eggs. Unvaccinated flocks can lose 80 to 100% of birds within a week.

First response: Vaccinate all surviving birds and all unaffected flocks in the area immediately. Report to local livestock/veterinary authority. Remove and safely dispose of dead birds (burn or bury deeply).

Vaccination: I-2 thermotolerant Newcastle Disease vaccine is the most practical vaccine for smallholders. Critical advantages:
- Does NOT need cold chain for short periods (unlike most vaccines). Can be kept at ambient temperature for up to several weeks.
- Can be given as eye drops or nose drops (one drop per bird), or dissolved in water for drinking.
- Water vaccination: Mix the vaccine in clean, cool, chlorine-free water. Withhold water from birds for 2 hours before vaccination to ensure all birds drink and receive the vaccine.
- Schedule: All birds vaccinated at 2 to 3 weeks of age, and every 3 to 6 months thereafter (more frequently in high-risk areas).
- Vaccinate the entire flock at the same time -- partial vaccination is less effective.

---

### Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza (HPAI / Bird Flu)

HPAI (commonly called Bird Flu) is a highly contagious and severe viral disease of poultry. H5N1 and other highly pathogenic strains can cause near-total mortality in poultry flocks very quickly. HPAI is a ZOONOTIC disease -- certain strains can infect and kill humans.

Symptoms: Sudden mass death of birds with no prior symptoms. In slightly slower outbreaks: sudden drop in egg production, respiratory distress, blue-purple discolouration of the comb and wattles (the fleshy parts on the head), swelling of the head and face, neurological signs (tremors, inability to stand), watery green diarrhoea.

IMPORTANT: REPORT TO AUTHORITIES IMMEDIATELY. HPAI is a notifiable disease of the highest priority. Do not handle dead birds without protection -- wear gloves, mask, and eye protection. Do not sell sick birds. Do not slaughter and eat sick birds. Culling of all birds on the farm is the standard response in most countries.

Prevention:
- Keep wild birds away from poultry housing. Wild waterfowl (ducks, geese) are a primary reservoir.
- Do not allow poultry to share water sources with wild birds.
- Biosecurity: limit access to poultry areas.
- Report any unexplained mass deaths immediately.

---

### Infectious Bursal Disease (Gumboro)

Gumboro is a highly contagious viral disease of chickens, most serious in chicks aged 3 to 6 weeks. The virus destroys the bursa of Fabricius -- an organ that produces immune cells -- leaving young birds immunodeficient and vulnerable to other infections.

Symptoms: Depression, loss of appetite, ruffled feathers, white watery diarrhoea, huddling together near a heat source, high mortality in young chicks. Chicks that survive may have a permanently weakened immune system.

Vaccination: Live attenuated Gumboro vaccine given to chicks at 14 to 18 days of age (via drinking water). Timing is critical -- vaccinate too early and maternal antibodies block the vaccine; too late and birds may already be infected. Follow the vaccine manufacturer or vet recommendation for your area.

---

### Marek's Disease

A herpesvirus disease of chickens causing progressive paralysis, weight loss, and internal tumours. Very widespread globally.

Symptoms: Progressive paralysis -- often one leg stretched forward and one stretched back (a characteristic sign). Tumours in internal organs. Weight loss. Skin tumours in some birds. Death.

Vaccination: Marek's Disease vaccine is given at the hatchery to day-old chicks. It cannot be given to older birds -- the window for effective vaccination is within 24 hours of hatching. Smallholders hatching their own chicks can vaccinate day-old chicks with guidance from a vet.

---

### Coccidiosis

Coccidiosis is caused by intestinal protozoan parasites (Eimeria species). It is the most important disease of young commercial chickens worldwide and is also serious in village chickens on dirty or wet ground.

Symptoms: Bloody diarrhoea (ranging from red-streaked to dark red-brown and watery), droopiness, ruffled feathers, loss of appetite, high mortality in young chicks (2 to 6 weeks old). Birds that survive may have poor feed conversion and growth.

First response: Immediate treatment with amprolium (Amprolium, Coccidiovit) or sulfonamide drugs dissolved in drinking water. Treat the entire flock, not just sick birds. Replace wet litter.

Prevention: Keep litter dry -- wet litter dramatically increases coccidial spread. Clean and disinfect housing between batches. Use coccidiostats in starter feed for commercial chicks. Do not overcrowd.

---

### Infectious Coryza

A bacterial respiratory disease of chickens (Avibacterium paragallinarum).

Symptoms: Facial swelling (especially around the sinuses under the eye), foul-smelling nasal discharge, sneezing, watery eyes, reduced appetite, drop in egg production. Not highly fatal but spreads through the flock and causes significant production losses.

First response: Antibiotic treatment (sulfonamides, erythromycin, oxytetracycline) prescribed by a vet. Treat the whole flock. Cull chronic carriers.

Prevention: Maintain strict biosecurity. Do not mix birds of different ages. Vaccinate in problem flocks (vaccines available in some countries).

---

### External Parasites in Poultry

External parasites cause significant production losses in village poultry.

Red Mite (Dermanyssus gallinae): Lives in cracks in the housing and feeds on birds at night. Signs: birds are restless at night, anaemic, show reduced production. Inspect housing at night with a torch -- mites are red when full of blood. Treatment: thorough cleaning of housing and application of acaricide to all cracks and surfaces (not directly to birds).

Lice: Lice live on the bird permanently. Signs: feather damage, excessive preening, skin irritation, anaemia in heavy infestations. Treatment: dust or spray birds with appropriate insecticide powder (e.g., carbaryl, permethrin). Repeat treatment after 2 weeks to kill newly hatched lice.

## 5. VACCINATION SCHEDULE OVERVIEW

Vaccination schedules vary by country and local disease pressure. Always confirm with your district veterinary office which diseases are present in your area and which vaccines are recommended. The schedules below are typical guides for smallholders.

### Village Chickens (Newcastle Disease focus)

- Day 14 to 21: Newcastle Disease (ND) I-2 vaccine -- eye/nose drops or drinking water method.
- Day 14 to 18: Gumboro (IBD) vaccine -- drinking water (if commercially relevant).
- Repeat ND vaccine every 3 months in high-risk areas.
- Vaccinate entire flock at same time.
- Keep birds off feed 2 hours before water vaccination (so they are thirsty and will drink).
- Use clean, chlorine-free water for water vaccination.
- Do not expose the vaccine to direct sunlight.

### Cattle (East Africa typical programme)

- 3-monthly: Acaricide treatment / dipping (more frequently in tick season).
- 6-monthly: FMD vaccine (check which strains are circulating with your district vet).
- Annual: LSD (Lumpy Skin Disease) vaccine.
- Annual: Blackleg (multi-clostridial) vaccine.
- Female calves aged 3 to 8 months (once only): Brucella vaccination (S19 or RB51) in national programmes.
- ECF immunisation: once, if available and affordable in your area.
- Rabies: annual in endemic areas, especially for dogs and cattle that could be exposed.

### Goats and Sheep

- Annual: PPR vaccine. Vaccinate all animals in the flock at the same time.
- Annual: Goat Pox or Sheep Pox vaccine (endemic areas).
- 3-monthly: FAMACHA worm check. Treat only animals that are anaemic (FAMACHA score 3, 4, or 5). Do not treat the whole flock.
- Orf vaccine: use in flocks with repeated annual outbreaks.

## 6. WHEN TO CALL A VETERINARIAN -- DECISION GUIDE

Not every sick animal needs a vet immediately. But certain signs demand immediate action. This guide helps farmers decide.

### Call Immediately (Same Hour)

- Mass death or multiple animals dying at once (especially poultry -- could be ND, HPAI; pigs -- could be ASF)
- Blisters on feet and inside the mouth of cattle, pigs, sheep, or goats (could be FMD)
- Pigs dying rapidly with bluish skin (could be ASF or CSF)
- Cattle with sudden high fever (40+ degrees Celsius) and swollen lymph nodes behind the ear (could be ECF)
- Multiple goats with fever, mouth ulcers, and severe diarrhoea (could be PPR)
- Cattle with sudden unexplained death and swollen legs with crepitation (could be Blackleg)
- Any animal aborting -- especially cattle (could be Brucellosis)

### Call Same Day

- Individual animal with fever above 40 degrees Celsius that has lasted more than 12 hours
- Animal not eating for 2 or more days
- Difficulty breathing at rest (laboured breathing, extended neck, open-mouth breathing)
- Difficult birth (dystocia) that is not progressing
- Wound that is infected (swollen, smelly, has pus)

### Treat and Monitor Yourself (Low urgency)

- Minor wounds or cuts (clean and apply wound spray)
- Mild lameness without swelling or fever
- Single animal coughing without fever
- External parasites (ticks, lice, mange)

### Warning Signs in ALL Species

These signs in any animal warrant at least close monitoring and usually a vet call:
- Temperature above 39.5 degrees Celsius (normal is around 38.5 to 39.5 degrees in most livestock)
- Not eating for more than 24 hours
- Dull, sunken eyes
- Isolation from the herd or flock -- sick animals instinctively separate themselves
- Laboured, rapid, or irregular breathing
- Unusual discharge from eyes, nose, mouth, or reproductive organs
- Sudden behaviour change (aggression, restlessness, staggering, paralysis)

## 7. QUARANTINE PROCEDURES FOR SMALLHOLDERS

Quarantine means separating animals to prevent disease spreading from one group to another. It is one of the most effective and lowest-cost disease control measures available.

### Quarantine for New Animals

Any animal entering your farm from outside -- bought at market, borrowed from a neighbour, or received as a gift -- may be carrying a disease even if it looks healthy. New animals must be isolated from your existing herd or flock for a minimum of 21 days (3 weeks) before mixing.

During quarantine:
- Keep the new animal in a separate pen or enclosure, out of sight and smell of the existing animals if possible.
- Use separate feeding and watering equipment.
- Handle the new animal after the existing herd, not before.
- Observe daily for any signs of illness.
- If you are purchasing breeding animals, request a health certificate from a vet.

### Quarantine for Sick Animals

Separate sick animals immediately when you notice they are unwell. Disease spreads fast in confined spaces, through shared water troughs, feed, and direct contact.

Key rules:
- Move the sick animal to a separate pen or enclosure as soon as you notice symptoms.
- Use separate feeding and watering equipment for the sick animal.
- Wash hands thoroughly after handling the sick animal before touching healthy animals.
- Disinfect footwear when moving between the sick animal and the healthy herd.
- Notify your vet or local livestock officer if the cause is unknown or if more animals start showing the same signs.

### Disposal of Dead Animals

How you dispose of dead animals matters enormously. Many diseases survive in carcasses for hours, days, or weeks.

- DO NOT leave carcasses in the field -- predators and scavengers spread disease to other farms.
- DO NOT sell dead or sick animals at market -- this is the single most common way disease spreads across a region.
- DO NOT slaughter and eat animals that have died of disease, especially ASF, FMD, Brucellosis, HPAI, or any disease you cannot identify -- this is a health risk to your family.
- Burn or bury carcasses deeply (at least 1 to 1.5 metres deep) away from water sources.
- Report any unusual deaths to the local veterinary authority.

### Notifiable Diseases

These diseases must by law be reported to the government veterinary authority in most countries:
- Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD)
- Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza (HPAI / Bird Flu)
- African Swine Fever (ASF)
- Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR)
- Classical Swine Fever (CSF)
- Brucellosis
- Rabies
- Newcastle Disease (in commercial settings)

Reporting is not just a legal requirement -- it triggers a government response that provides resources, helps contain the outbreak, and often compensates farmers who lose animals. Failure to report leaves you and your neighbours without support.

## 8. BIOSECURITY BASICS FOR SMALLHOLDERS

Biosecurity means all the practices that prevent disease from entering your farm and spreading between animals. Many of the most effective biosecurity measures cost nothing.

**Keep feed stores secure.** Rodents (rats, mice) carry disease organisms and contaminate feed. Keep feed in sealed metal or plastic bins. Rats and mice also carry leptospirosis, salmonella, and other diseases that can affect livestock and humans.

**Prevent mixing with unknown animals.** Markets are where disease spreads most rapidly. If you buy animals at market, quarantine them before mixing. If you allow your animals to graze on common land or alongside neighbours' animals, disease sharing is continuous.

**Clean and disinfect housing between flocks.** For poultry especially: remove all litter, scrub the floor, walls, and perches with detergent, then apply a disinfectant (lime wash or commercial disinfectant), and rest the housing for at least 2 weeks before introducing new birds.

**Limit visitor access to animal areas.** Veterinarians, traders, and neighbours can carry disease on their clothing and footwear. Provide foot dip (shallow tray with disinfectant) at the entrance to pens. Ask visitors to wear clean boots or plastic bags over their shoes.

**Wash hands before and after handling animals.** This protects both you (from zoonotic diseases) and the animals (from pathogens you may carry from other farms or markets).

**Keep wild birds away from poultry.** Wild waterfowl (ducks, geese, egrets) are the natural reservoir for avian influenza viruses. They shed the virus in their droppings without becoming sick themselves. Cover poultry runs with netting. Do not allow poultry to share water sources with wild birds.

**For pigs: never feed kitchen waste or food scraps.** This is the most important pig biosecurity rule. Kitchen waste (swill) -- even if cooked -- is the primary route of spread for African Swine Fever and Classical Swine Fever globally. Pigs can only be fed purpose-grown feed or agricultural by-products that have not been in contact with meat products.

**Early reporting saves more animals than late reporting.** The sooner a disease is identified and contained, the fewer animals are lost. There is no penalty for reporting a disease and it turns out to be something minor. But failing to report a serious disease means it spreads to other farms, and you lose the chance of government compensation and support.
