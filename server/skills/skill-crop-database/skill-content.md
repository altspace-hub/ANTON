# Thematic Skill: Crop Knowledge Database

This skill gives you comprehensive, practical knowledge of major crops grown across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. Use it to advise smallholder farmers, extension workers, and agricultural programme staff on planting seasons, soil and water requirements, common pests and diseases, expected yields, and market pricing factors. All information is written in plain, practical language.

---

## 1. CEREAL CROPS

### Maize (Corn)

Maize is the most widely grown staple food crop in sub-Saharan Africa and a major food and feed crop in South and Southeast Asia.

**Planting seasons by region:**
- West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Cote d'Ivoire): Long rains March to April. Short rains September to October.
- East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda): Long rains March to May. Short rains October to November.
- Southern Africa (Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, Mozambique): Single main season November to January (planting). Harvest April to June.
- South Asia (India, Bangladesh): Kharif (monsoon) season June to July planting, harvested September to October.
- Southeast Asia (Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam): May to June main planting.

**Soil requirements:** Maize prefers well-drained loam or sandy loam soils. Ideal soil pH is 5.5 to 7.0. Strongly acidic soils below pH 5.5 reduce nutrient availability and need lime treatment.

**Water needs:** Maize requires 500 to 800 mm of rainfall spread across the growing season, or equivalent irrigation. Water stress during flowering (tasselling and silking, around 60 to 70 days after planting) is the most damaging.

**Key pests:**
- **Fall Armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda)** -- The most critical pest of maize in Africa since its arrival in 2016. A caterpillar that eats into the maize whorl and can destroy young plants rapidly. Signs: ragged holes in leaves, wet sawdust-like droppings in the whorl, damaged growing tip. Control: apply sand or fine ash into the whorl for small infestations; approved insecticides (emamectin benzoate, spinosad) for serious infestations.
- **Maize Stalk Borer (Busseola fusca and Chilo partellus)** -- caterpillar tunnels inside the stem. Signs: dead heart (central shoot dies), breakage at stem.
- **Aphids** -- cluster on leaves and under husks, suck sap, and transmit Maize Lethal Necrosis (MLN). Control with insecticide sprays or encourage natural predators.
- **Rodents and birds** -- serious problem at germination and at harvest.

**Key diseases:**
- **Maize Lethal Necrosis (MLN)** -- Devastating viral disease present in East Africa, especially Kenya. Caused by two viruses together (MCMV plus another). Symptoms: yellowing starting from leaf edges moving inward, premature drying of leaves, small or no cobs. No treatment once infected. Prevention: use certified MLN-tolerant seed, control aphids, remove and destroy infected plants.
- **Grey Leaf Spot** -- Fungal. Symptoms: rectangular grey-brown lesions on leaves following the leaf veins. Use resistant varieties, improve air circulation.
- **Northern Corn Blight** -- Fungal. Large cigar-shaped grey-green lesions on leaves. Fungicide sprays help if applied early.

**Expected yields:**
- Subsistence farming (local or recycled seed, no fertiliser): 1 to 2 t/ha.
- Improved practices (improved variety plus fertiliser): 3 to 6 t/ha.
- Commercial farming (certified hybrid seed plus full fertiliser plus irrigation): 6 to 10 t/ha.

**Market notes:** Maize prices are lowest at harvest time and highest in the lean season three to four months before the next harvest. Farmers who can store maize and sell later typically earn 30 to 60% more. Hermetic bags (sealed plastic bags that cut off oxygen) effectively protect stored maize from weevils without chemicals.

### Rice

Rice is the primary staple crop across South and Southeast Asia and is increasingly important in West Africa. Two main growing systems: lowland paddy (flooded) and upland (rain-fed, unflooded).

**Key varieties and systems:**
- **Lowland paddy rice:** Grown in flooded fields (paddies). Requires standing water for much of the growing period. Higher yields but needs water management infrastructure.
- **Upland rice:** Grown on well-drained slopes without flooding. Lower yields but accessible to farmers without irrigation.
- **Dry season rice with irrigation:** East Africa and South Asia can produce a second crop in the dry season.

**Planting seasons:**
- South Asia: Kharif season -- transplant June to July, harvest October to November. Rabi (irrigated dry season) -- plant November to December, harvest March to April.
- West Africa: Main season follows the rains -- transplant June to July, harvest October to November.
- Southeast Asia: Two to three crops per year in irrigated areas.

**Water needs:** Lowland paddy requires continuous flooding (5 to 10 cm water depth). Upland rice requires 1,000 to 1,500 mm of well-distributed rainfall.

**Key pests:**
- **Brown Planthopper** -- Sucking insect, attacks base of plant, causes hopper burn (sudden yellowing and drying of patches). Serious in irrigated rice in South and Southeast Asia. Pesticide resistance is widespread.
- **Stem Borer** -- Caterpillar enters the stem. Causes dead heart in vegetative stage; white ear (empty panicle) in reproductive stage.
- **Rats** -- Extremely serious pre-harvest pest. Community-organised rat control (baiting, trapping) is more effective than individual effort.

**Key diseases:**
- **Rice Blast (Pyricularia oryzae)** -- Most critical rice disease globally. Affects leaves (diamond-shaped grey lesions with brown borders) and neck (neck rot causes the panicle to break and grain to be lost). Favoured by cool nights, morning dew, and high nitrogen fertiliser. Use resistant varieties, avoid excess nitrogen, apply fungicide (tricyclazole, azoxystrobin) at booting and early flowering if blast is present.
- **Bacterial Leaf Blight (BLB)** -- Yellowing of leaf margins starting from the tip. No effective chemical treatment -- use resistant varieties.
- **Sheath Blight** -- Irregular grey-green lesions on the sheath. Spreads at high plant density. Fungicide (validamycin) effective.

**Expected yields:**
- Traditional varieties, no fertiliser: 1.5 to 2 t/ha (paddy weight, to be milled).
- Improved high-yielding varieties (HYV) with fertiliser: 3 to 6 t/ha.
- Well-managed irrigated commercial rice: up to 8 t/ha per crop.

**Market notes:** Rice is sold as paddy (unhusked) at the farm gate or as milled white rice at market. Milling removes 20 to 30% of weight but multiplies the price significantly. Seasonal storage from wet season harvest to dry season scarcity can double the price received.

### Wheat

Wheat is a cool-season cereal grown in higher-altitude and temperate areas of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Not a smallholder crop in hot lowlands.

**Where it grows:** Ethiopian and Kenyan highlands (above 1,500 m), Zimbabwean and Zambian plateau areas, Pakistan and North India, Nepal highlands.

**Planting seasons:**
- East Africa highlands: Sown June to July at altitude; harvested October to November.
- Southern Africa highlands: Sown June to July (cool dry season with irrigation), harvested October to November.
- South Asia: Rabi crop -- sown October to November, harvested March to April.

**Soil requirements:** Deep, well-drained loam soils, pH 6.0 to 7.5. Wheat does not tolerate waterlogging.

**Water needs:** 400 to 600 mm during the season. Irrigation is common in South Asia.

**Key pests:**
- **Wheat Aphid** -- Clusters on leaves and stems, causes yellowing, transmits Barley Yellow Dwarf Virus. Control with systemic insecticides if threshold exceeded.
- **Hessian Fly** -- Larvae feed at the base of the plant, weakening stems. Use resistant varieties and plant after the fly-free date in affected areas.

**Key diseases:**
- **Stem Rust (Puccinia graminis)** -- Reddish-brown pustules on stems and leaves. Can cause near-total yield loss. Ug99 race from Uganda and Kenya is a major international concern. Use currently resistant varieties. Fungicide (propiconazole, tebuconazole) applied at early signs is effective.
- **Yellow Rust (Stripe Rust)** -- Yellow stripes of pustules along the leaf. Cool and wet conditions favour it. Critical in Ethiopian and Kenyan highlands. Same fungicide control as stem rust.
- **Septoria Leaf Blotch** -- Tan blotches with yellow halos on leaves. Fungicide effective.

**Expected yields:** 2 to 4 t/ha in well-managed smallholder conditions in African highlands. Up to 5 to 6 t/ha in irrigated South Asian conditions.

### Pearl Millet

Pearl millet is a dryland staple crop critical for food security in the West African Sahel and semi-arid regions of South Asia. One of the most drought-tolerant and heat-tolerant cereals available to smallholders.

**Where it grows:** West Africa Sahel (Niger, Mali, Senegal, northern Nigeria, Burkina Faso), dry zones of East Africa, Rajasthan and Gujarat in India.

**Planting season:** West Africa: June to July when rains begin. South Asia: June to July (kharif).

**Soil requirements:** Sandy soils, poor in organic matter -- millet tolerates soils that maize or wheat would fail in entirely. pH 5.5 to 7.0. Does not tolerate waterlogging.

**Water needs:** 250 to 500 mm. Can survive short drought periods that would kill other cereals.

**Key pests:**
- **Millet Head Miner** -- Caterpillar feeds inside the developing head. Signs: whitish webbing on the head, broken spikelets. Use early-maturing varieties to escape peak infestation.
- **Locusts** -- Periodic invasions can devastate entire fields. FAO and national early warning systems trigger coordinated response.
- **Birds** -- Extremely serious in Sahel. Millet fields require continuous bird-scaring during grain fill.

**Key diseases:**
- **Downy Mildew (Sclerospora graminicola)** -- Stunted plants, pale green or yellow colour, white powdery growth on undersides of leaves, witch's broom malformation of the head. Can destroy 30 to 50% of crop in affected areas. Use resistant varieties (available from ICRISAT).
- **Ergot** -- Honeydew exuded from the head, followed by hard black or dark purple bodies replacing the grain. Avoid planting saved seed from infected fields.

**Expected yields:** 0.5 to 1.5 t/ha under rain-fed conditions without fertiliser. Up to 3 t/ha with improved varieties and fertiliser.

**Market notes:** Pearl millet typically fetches a lower market price than maize but serves as an essential drought insurance crop -- when maize fails, millet often partially survives.

### Sorghum

Sorghum is a dual-purpose crop (grain for food and beer, stems for fodder and building material) grown across semi-arid Africa and South Asia. More drought-tolerant than maize.

**Where it grows:** East Africa (Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Sudan), West Africa (Nigeria, Burkina Faso, Mali), Southern Africa dryland areas, India (Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Karnataka), Myanmar.

**Planting season:** Follows the local rains. East Africa: March to May and October to November. Southern Africa: November to January. India: June to July (kharif).

**Soil requirements:** Tolerates clay soils and moderately poor soils. pH 5.5 to 7.5. More tolerant of temporary waterlogging than maize.

**Water needs:** 400 to 650 mm. Drought tolerance is excellent -- sorghum goes into dormancy under water stress and resumes growth when water returns.

**Key pests:**
- **Shoot Fly (Atherigona soccata)** -- Most serious pest in South Asia. Larvae bore into shoot and cause dead heart. Early sowing avoids peak fly populations.
- **Stalk Borer** -- As in maize.
- **Birds** -- Sorghum's open head makes it highly vulnerable to bird damage at ripening. Covered-head varieties reduce losses.
- **Sorghum Midge** -- Destroys flowers during pollination. Use resistant varieties or early or late planting to miss peak midge flight.

**Key diseases:**
- **Head Smut** -- Entire sorghum head replaced by a bag of black spores. Treat seed with fungicide (thiram, carboxin) before planting.
- **Anthracnose** -- Red or orange lesions on leaves, lesions on grain. Use resistant varieties.

**Expected yields:** 1 to 3 t/ha under rain-fed conditions with improved varieties.

## 2. ROOT AND TUBER CROPS

### Cassava

Cassava is a critical food security crop across sub-Saharan Africa. It tolerates poor soils and drought, can be left in the ground for up to two years, and provides calories when other crops fail. Grown from stem cuttings, not seeds.

**Where it grows:** Humid and sub-humid zones of West, Central, and East Africa (Nigeria is the world's largest producer). Also Southeast Asia (Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam).

**Planting season:** Start of or during the rains. West Africa: April to June. East Africa: March to May or October to November.

**Soil requirements:** Sandy loam to loam soils. Does surprisingly well on poor, acidic soils (pH 4.5 to 7.0). Poorly drained or waterlogged soils reduce yield dramatically.

**Water needs:** 1,000 to 1,500 mm per year, but drought tolerant -- cassava survives dry spells by shedding leaves and resuming growth when rain returns.

**Key pests:**
- **Cassava Mealybug (Phenacoccus manihoti)** -- White, cottony insect clusters on growing tips, causes stunting and bunchy top. Now well controlled in most areas by the introduced biological control agent Anagyrus lopezi.
- **Cassava Green Mite (Mononychellus tanajoa)** -- Tiny mite, feeds on young leaves, causes angular yellowing. Use mite-resistant varieties.

**Key diseases:**
- **Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD)** -- Most devastating cassava disease in Africa. Symptoms: yellow and green mosaic pattern on leaves, leaf distortion, severe stunting. Spread by whitefly and through infected cuttings. No treatment once infected. Prevention: plant CMD-resistant varieties; use clean, disease-free cuttings; rogue and destroy infected plants early.
- **Cassava Brown Streak Disease (CBSD)** -- Serious and spreading disease in East and Central Africa. Symptoms: yellow streaks along leaf veins, brown corky rot inside the cassava root making it inedible. A field may look healthy outside but have rotted roots at harvest. Use CBSD-tolerant varieties, obtain clean cuttings from tested sources.

**Expected yields:** 10 to 20 t/ha fresh root weight with improved, disease-free varieties and good management. Many smallholders get 5 to 10 t/ha under poor conditions.

**Market notes:** Fresh cassava roots are highly perishable -- they begin to deteriorate within 24 to 48 hours of harvest. Processing into dried chips, flour, or fermented gari or fufu significantly extends shelf life and adds value.

### Yam

Yams are a culturally important staple food in West Africa, particularly in Nigeria, Ghana, Cote d'Ivoire, and Benin (the Yam Belt). Labour-intensive and require fertile, well-drained soils.

**Where it grows:** Primarily West Africa (Guinea forest zone).

**Planting season:** West Africa: Plant February to March. Harvest July to September (early yams) and October to November (late yams).

**Soil requirements:** Deep, well-drained, fertile loam or sandy loam. Soils should be loosened to at least 30 cm depth to allow tuber development. pH 5.5 to 6.5.

**Water needs:** 1,200 to 1,500 mm, well distributed.

**Key pests:**
- **Yam Beetle (Heteroligus species)** -- Feeds on the tuber in the ground, causing holes and entry points for rot. Use clean land, avoid replanting in previously infested fields.
- **Nematodes** -- Root-knot nematodes cause galls on roots and small, misshapen tubers. Crop rotation and clean planting material help.

**Key diseases:**
- **Yam Anthracnose** -- Lesions on leaves (dark brown with yellow halo), cankers on stems, patches of rot on stored tubers. Use resistant varieties, clean stakes, and fungicide on planting material.
- **Dry Rot (Rhizoctonia and Sclerotium)** -- Stored yam rots from inside. Good curing of harvested yams (leaving in the field for a week to harden the skin) reduces rot.

**Expected yields:** 8 to 15 t/ha fresh weight in well-managed smallholder plots.

**Market notes:** Yam prices are highly seasonal -- low at harvest, high in the months before the new harvest (February to May). Yam is one of Africa's highest-value food crops per unit of land.

### Sweet Potato

Sweet potato is a fast-growing, nutritious crop (especially orange-fleshed varieties high in vitamin A). Matures in three to four months.

**Where it grows:** Widespread across East Africa (Uganda is a major producer), West Africa, and South and Southeast Asia.

**Planting season:** Can be planted at any time with adequate moisture. Typically planted at the start of the rains.

**Soil requirements:** Well-drained sandy loam. pH 5.5 to 6.5. Excess nitrogen promotes leaf growth at the expense of the root.

**Key pests:**
- **Sweet Potato Weevil (Cylas formicarius)** -- Most critical pest. Adults and larvae bore into vines and roots, causing a foul smell and bitter taste that makes roots unfit for eating. Infested roots appear fine on the outside. Prevention: use clean, weevil-free vines; harvest on time; cover exposed roots with soil (hilling); crop rotation.

**Key diseases:**
- **Viral diseases (Sweet Potato Virus Disease complex)** -- Spread by aphids and whitefly. Symptoms: leaf distortion, mosaic patterns, severe stunting, poor root development. Use certified, virus-tested vines from clean sources.
- **Black Rot (Ceratocystis fimbriata)** -- Dark sunken lesions on stored roots. Cure harvested roots properly (warm, humid conditions for 4 to 7 days) and store in cool, dry conditions.

**Expected yields:** 6 to 12 t/ha fresh weight with improved orange-fleshed varieties and good management.

### Irish Potato

Irish potato is a high-value vegetable and food crop in the cool highlands of East Africa (Kenya, Uganda, Ethiopia, Rwanda) and in South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Nepal).

**Where it grows:** East African highlands at 1,500 to 3,000 m altitude. South Asian Rabi season. Highland Zimbabwe.

**Planting season:**
- East Africa highlands: Long rains (March to April planting, harvest June to July) and short rains (September to October planting, harvest December to January).
- South Asia: Rabi season -- October to November planting, February to March harvest.

**Soil requirements:** Deep, well-drained loam, rich in organic matter. pH 5.0 to 6.5.

**Key pests:**
- **Potato Tuber Moth (Phthorimaea operculella)** -- Larvae tunnel into tubers. Very damaging in storage if not managed. Control: earth up plants to cover tubers, harvest before soil dries and cracks, store in a cool place.
- **Aphids** -- Transmit potato viruses. Use insecticide sprays if threshold is exceeded.

**Key diseases:**
- **Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)** -- The most critical and dangerous potato disease globally. Water-soaked patches on leaves that rapidly turn brown and black in cool, wet weather. Can destroy an entire crop in 7 to 10 days. Prevention: use certified blight-resistant varieties; apply fungicide (chlorothalonil, mancozeb as protectant; metalaxyl-M as systemic) on a 7 to 10 day spray schedule; scout daily in cool and wet weather; remove and destroy infected plants.
- **Early Blight (Alternaria solani)** -- Dark brown concentric ring lesions on older leaves. Fungicide sprays control it.
- **Bacterial Wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum)** -- Plants wilt suddenly, brown discolouration inside the stem. No treatment -- destroy infected plants, do not replant potatoes in that soil for at least three years. Use clean, certified seed potatoes.

**Expected yields:** 8 to 20 t/ha fresh tubers with good management. Yields below 5 t/ha are common where late blight is uncontrolled.

**Market notes:** Potatoes are highly perishable. Cold storage (4 to 8 degrees Celsius) can extend shelf life to four to six months, allowing off-season premium pricing. Seed potato production is a highly profitable enterprise in highland areas.

## 3. LEGUMES

### Common Bean (Kidney Bean, Snap Bean)

Common beans are the most important food legume in East and Central Africa. Often intercropped with maize. Beans fix atmospheric nitrogen and improve soil fertility.

**Where it grows:** East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Ethiopia), Central Africa, parts of South Asia.

**Planting season:** Follows the maize planting season -- planted at the same time in intercrops, or in the short rains when maize is not grown.

**Soil requirements:** Well-drained loam to sandy loam. pH 6.0 to 7.0. Sensitive to waterlogging.

**Key pests:**
- **Bean Stem Maggot (Ophiomyia species)** -- Fly larvae bore into the stem at soil level, causing yellowing and death of young plants. Apply seed treatment insecticide (imidacloprid) or granular insecticide at planting.
- **Pod Borer (Maruca vitrata)** -- Caterpillar enters the pod and feeds on developing seeds. Insecticide spray at flowering and early pod stage.
- **Aphids** -- Colonise growing tips and undersides of leaves. Transmit bean mosaic virus. Remove heavily colonised tips; spray insecticide if severe.

**Key diseases:**
- **Bean Common Mosaic Virus (BCMV)** -- Mosaic leaf patterns, leaf distortion, stunting. Spread by aphids and infected seed. Use certified virus-free seed, plant resistant varieties, control aphids.
- **Angular Leaf Spot (Phaeoisariopsis griseola)** -- Angular brown lesions on leaves, spots on pods, premature defoliation. Favoured by cool, humid conditions. Use resistant varieties, copper-based fungicide.
- **Anthracnose** -- Dark, sunken lesions on pods, stems, and seeds. Spread by infected seed and rain splash. Use disease-free certified seed.

**Expected yields:** 0.5 to 1.5 t/ha dry bean weight under smallholder conditions. Up to 2.5 t/ha with improved varieties and full management.

### Groundnut (Peanut)

Groundnuts are a vital source of protein, cooking oil, and cash income for smallholders across West Africa, East Africa, and South Asia. The nuts develop underground.

**Where it grows:** West Africa (Nigeria, Senegal, Sudan, Burkina Faso), East Africa (Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi), South Asia (India, Myanmar).

**Planting season:** Follows the rains -- May to July in West Africa; March to May and October to November in East Africa.

**Soil requirements:** Sandy loam to loam, well-drained. Heavy clay reduces pegging (the process by which the fertilised flower bends into the soil to form the pod). pH 5.5 to 7.0. Requires calcium in the soil at the pegging zone -- apply gypsum if calcium is lacking.

**Water needs:** 500 to 700 mm, well distributed. Drought at pegging and pod filling is most damaging.

**Key pests:**
- **Aphids (Aphis craccivora)** -- Transmit groundnut rosette virus. Control aphids to protect against rosette.
- **Thrips** -- Feeding scars on leaves, transmit viruses.

**Key diseases:**
- **Groundnut Rosette Virus** -- The most important groundnut disease in West Africa. Causes severe stunting, yellow and green mosaic on leaves, massive yield loss. Spread exclusively by the groundnut aphid. Prevention: early planting before aphid peak, close plant spacing, use resistant or tolerant varieties.
- **Early Leaf Spot (Cercosporidium arachidicola)** -- Circular dark brown lesions with yellow halo on upper leaf surface. Causes defoliation. Fungicide (chlorothalonil, mancozeb) on 14-day schedule if severe.
- **Late Leaf Spot (Phaeoisariopsis personata)** -- Similar but darker lesions on underside of leaf. More severe than early spot.
- **Aflatoxin contamination (Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus)** -- A critical food safety and market problem. A fungal mould that produces aflatoxin -- a potent carcinogen and liver toxin. The mould colonises pods in the soil when drought stress occurs near harvest. Signs: mouldy, shrivelled pods; greenish-yellow powder inside shells. High-aflatoxin groundnuts are rejected from export and cause liver cancer and child stunting with chronic exposure. Prevention: harvest on time; dry harvested nuts quickly to below 8% moisture; discard visibly mouldy nuts; use biocontrol (Aflasafe) where available.

**Expected yields:** 0.8 to 2 t/ha shelled nuts with improved varieties and disease management.

**Market notes:** Groundnut value depends heavily on aflatoxin levels. Export-market buyers strictly test for aflatoxin and reject above 10 to 20 ppb. Processing into peanut butter adds value but requires equipment access.

### Cowpea

Cowpea (black-eyed pea) is a heat and drought-tolerant legume, important for food security in West Africa and the Sahel. Dual-purpose: leaves and green pods eaten as vegetable; dried seeds are high-protein food.

**Where it grows:** West Africa Sahel (Nigeria, Niger, Senegal, Burkina Faso), East Africa, South Asia (India).

**Planting season:** Follows the rains -- June to July in West Africa; March to May in East Africa.

**Soil requirements:** Sandy soils, tolerates low fertility. pH 6.0 to 7.0. Does not tolerate waterlogging.

**Key pests:**
- **Flower Thrips (Megalurothrips sjostedti)** -- Feeds inside flowers, causes flower abortion, greatly reduces pod set. Most critical pest of cowpea. Apply insecticide at early flowering.
- **Pod Borer (Maruca vitrata)** -- Same as in common bean.
- **Bruchid Weevil (Callosobruchus maculatus)** -- Most important storage pest. Small grey-brown beetle lays eggs on dried pods in the field; larvae develop inside the seed during storage; weight loss can be 30 to 50% in three to six months. Prevention: hermetic storage (oxygen-free bags) kills larvae without chemicals.

**Key diseases:**
- **Cowpea Mosaic Virus** -- Mosaic and distortion of leaves. Spread by aphids. Use resistant varieties, control aphids.
- **Cercospora Leaf Spot** -- Brown circular spots on leaves. Fungicide effective.

**Expected yields:** 0.5 to 1.5 t/ha dry grain under rain-fed conditions.

### Soybean

Soybean is growing rapidly as a smallholder crop in East Africa and is established in South and Southeast Asia.

**Where it grows:** East and Southern Africa (Ethiopia, Uganda, Tanzania, Zambia). South Asia (India). Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Myanmar, Thailand).

**Planting season:** East Africa: March to May and October to November. South Asia: June to July (kharif). Southeast Asia: May to June.

**Soil requirements:** Loam soils, pH 6.0 to 7.0. Benefits strongly from inoculation with Bradyrhizobium japonicum bacteria applied as a coating on the seed just before planting. Without inoculation, soybean cannot fix nitrogen and yields will be poor. Always inoculate seed if planting in a new area.

**Key pests:**
- **Bean Stem Fly** -- As in common bean.
- **Stink Bugs (various)** -- Feed on pods and seeds, causing seed shrivelling. Insecticide sprays at pod fill.

**Key diseases:**
- **Soybean Rust (Phakopsora pachyrhizi)** -- Small tan-brown lesions on undersides of leaves, rapid defoliation. Can cause very large yield losses. Fungicide (triazoles -- tebuconazole, propiconazole) applied at first sign of lesion or preventively in high-risk areas.
- **Bacterial Pustule** -- Small raised spots on leaves with yellow halo. Generally causes limited damage.

**Expected yields:** 1 to 3 t/ha with inoculation, good management, and disease control.

**Market notes:** Soybean demand is growing rapidly, driven by the poultry and livestock feed sector across Africa. Contract growing arrangements with feed millers are available in some areas.

## 4. CASH AND EXPORT CROPS

### Coffee (Arabica)

Arabica coffee is a high-value export crop grown in the highland areas of East Africa, particularly Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, and Burundi.

**Where it grows:** East Africa highlands at 1,400 to 2,000 m altitude. Full sun or shade-grown under trees.

**Planting season:** Coffee is a perennial tree crop. Peak harvest period: October to January in most East African countries.

**Soil requirements:** Deep, fertile, well-drained volcanic or loam soils. Rich in organic matter. pH 6.0 to 6.5.

**Water needs:** 1,500 to 2,000 mm per year, well distributed.

**Key pests:**
- **Coffee Berry Borer (Hypothenemus hampei)** -- The most critical pest of coffee worldwide. A tiny black beetle bores into the coffee berry and lays eggs inside. Larvae develop inside the bean, destroying it. Signs: small circular holes in the berry. Control: strip harvesting (harvest all berries at once), expose bean waste to sun, use Beauveria bassiana biological control fungus as a spray.
- **Green Scale (Coccus viridis)** -- Soft scale insect on branches and leaves, causes sooty mould. Biological control agents effective.

**Key diseases:**
- **Coffee Leaf Rust (Hemileia vastatrix)** -- The most important coffee disease globally. Orange powdery pustules on the underside of leaves. Leaves drop, reducing photosynthesis and yield. Management: use resistant or tolerant varieties (Batian, Ruiru 11 in Kenya); apply copper-based fungicide preventively before the rains and after; maintain tree health with pruning and nutrition.
- **Coffee Berry Disease (CBD)** -- Critical disease in East Africa. Black sunken lesions on green berries, causing berry drop and shrivelled black beans inside. Huge economic impact. Control: use CBD-resistant varieties (Ruiru 11, Batian); copper fungicide sprays during green berry stage; strip harvesting to remove infected berries.

**Expected yields:** 0.5 to 2 t/ha cherry coffee, which reduces to approximately 0.1 to 0.4 t/ha green bean after processing and drying.

**Market notes:** Coffee quality determines price more than in almost any other crop. Specialty coffee earns two to five times the commodity price. Wet processing (washing) produces better quality and higher prices than dry processing. Certification (Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance, Organic, UTZ) provides price premiums but requires compliance costs.

### Cocoa

Cocoa is the source of chocolate. West Africa produces about 70% of the world's cocoa, with Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana as the two largest producers.

**Where it grows:** West Africa humid forest zone (Cote d'Ivoire, Ghana, Nigeria, Cameroon).

**Planting season:** Cocoa is a tree crop. Main harvest seasons: major crop (October to March), minor crop (May to August).

**Soil requirements:** Deep, well-drained, fertile loam soils under partial shade. pH 6.0 to 7.0.

**Water needs:** 1,500 to 2,000 mm per year. Tolerates a 2 to 3 month dry season.

**Key pests:**
- **Cocoa Mirid Bugs (Sahlbergella singularis and Distantiella theobroma)** -- Feed on pods, young shoots, and stems. Cause sudden wilting and blackening of shoots. Insecticide (lambda-cyhalothrin, deltamethrin) applied at the right time is effective.

**Key diseases:**
- **Black Pod Disease (Phytophthora palmivora and P. megakarya)** -- The most important cocoa disease in Africa. Black rot starting at any point on the pod, spreading rapidly, destroying the beans inside. In humid conditions, can cause 30 to 40% yield loss if uncontrolled. Control: copper fungicide sprays (copper oxychloride) every 3 weeks during wet season; remove and destroy all blackened pods; prune to improve air circulation; harvest frequently.
- **Cocoa Swollen Shoot Virus (CSSV)** -- Spread by mealybugs. Causes swelling of roots and shoots, mosaic on leaves, and progressive decline and death of the tree. Entire tree must be removed and destroyed. No other control.

**Expected yields:** 0.3 to 0.8 t/ha dry beans on smallholder farms in good condition.

**Market notes:** Certification premiums (Fair Trade, UTZ, Rainforest Alliance) are typically 150 to 200 USD per tonne above the commodity price. Fermentation and drying quality determines the premium paid at the buying station.

---

### Tea

Tea is a perennial plantation crop grown in the cool, humid highlands of East Africa, particularly Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda, and Tanzania.

**Where it grows:** East African highlands above 1,500 m. Very high rainfall and cool temperatures required.

**Soil requirements:** Deep, well-drained, acidic soils. pH 4.5 to 5.5. High organic matter.

**Key pests:**
- **Tea Mosquito Bug** -- Piercing-sucking insect, causes lesions on young shoots. Insecticide sprays.
- **Red Spider Mite** -- Causes bronzing of leaves, reduces photosynthesis. Miticide sprays.

**Key diseases:**
- **Blister Blight (Exobasidium vexans)** -- Pale blisters on young leaves that turn white and then collapse. Very damaging in wet, cloudy weather. Copper fungicide applied regularly.
- **Grey Blight (Pestalotiopsis species)** -- Tan-grey lesions on mature leaves, dieback of shoots. Copper fungicide.

**Expected yields:** 2 to 5 t/ha made tea per year from a well-maintained smallholder plot.

**Market notes:** Smallholder tea farmers in Kenya sell green leaf to KTDA (Kenya Tea Development Agency) factories. Specialty origin teas from Rwanda and Kenya earn premiums in Western markets.

---

### Cotton

Cotton is an important cash crop in West Africa, Southern Africa, and parts of South and East Africa.

**Where it grows:** Semi-arid zones with a well-defined growing season. West Africa (Burkina Faso, Mali, Cote d'Ivoire, Cameroon), Southern Africa (Zimbabwe, Zambia, Mozambique, Tanzania).

**Planting season:** West Africa: May to June. Southern Africa: November to January.

**Soil requirements:** Well-drained loam to clay-loam. pH 6.0 to 7.5.

**Water needs:** 700 to 1,300 mm. Drought during boll formation (approximately 70 to 90 days after planting) is most damaging.

**Key pests:**
- **Bollworm complex (Helicoverpa armigera and others)** -- The most critical pest of cotton globally. Larvae bore into bolls (the cotton pods) and eat the fibre and seeds. Require a precise spray programme based on scouting.
- **Sucking insects (jassids, aphids, whitefly, mired bugs)** -- Feed on leaves and growing tips, reduce growth and transmit diseases. Systemic insecticides effective.

**Key diseases:**
- **Bacterial Blight (Xanthomonas campestris)** -- Angular water-soaked lesions on leaves, stem cankers, boll infection. Use certified disease-free seed, resistant varieties.
- **Fusarium Wilt** -- Yellowing, wilting, and death of plants. Brown discolouration inside the stem. Soilborne fungus. Use resistant varieties, crop rotation.

**Expected yields:** 0.5 to 1.5 t/ha seed cotton. Ginning is done at industrial gins -- smallholders sell seed cotton.

**Market notes:** Cotton prices are linked to the world market and fluctuate significantly. Cotton is highly input-intensive -- the cost of inputs must be carefully compared to expected output price before planting.

## 5. VEGETABLES (KEY SMALLHOLDER CROPS)

### Tomato

Tomato is one of the highest-value crops a smallholder can grow but also one of the most difficult. It is highly susceptible to disease and the market is volatile.

Planting season: Dry season with irrigation preferred (better prices, fewer diseases). Can also be grown in the rains, but disease pressure is higher.

Soil requirements: Well-drained, fertile loam. Rich in organic matter. pH 6.0 to 7.0.

Key pests:
- Tuta absoluta (Tomato Leaf Miner): An invasive South American moth that arrived in Africa after 2008. Now the most critical tomato pest in much of Africa. Tiny caterpillars mine inside leaves and bore into fruits. Can destroy 80 to 100% of unprotected crop. Control requires pheromone traps to monitor, Bacillus thuringiensis spray for larvae, insecticide rotation to avoid resistance, and physical barriers.
- Aphids: Colonise growing tips, transmit viruses.
- Whitefly: Transmits Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus.

Key diseases:
- Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans): As in potato. Can destroy tomato crop in days in wet conditions. Preventive fungicide sprays essential.
- Bacterial Wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum): Sudden wilting of plants, no recovery. Remove and destroy infected plants. Use resistant varieties, avoid infested soil.
- Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV): Spread by whitefly. Leaves curl upward, plant stunted, little or no fruit. Use resistant varieties where available.

Expected yields: 15 to 40 t/ha fresh fruit with good management.

Market notes: Tomato prices crash at the peak of the rainy season when everyone harvests at once. Off-season production with irrigation is the key strategy for profitability. Tomatoes are highly perishable -- losses from farm to market can reach 30 to 50% without proper crates and rapid transport.

### Kale and Collard Greens (Sukuma Wiki)

Sukuma wiki is the most important leafy vegetable in East Africa.

Where it grows: East and Southern Africa, year-round.

Planting season: Can be grown year-round.

Key pests: Diamondback Moth (Plutella xylostella) -- small caterpillar, use Bt or spinosad.

Key diseases: Black Rot, Downy Mildew.

Market notes: Daily market crop, stable demand year-round.

### Onion

Onion is a high-value dry-season crop with irrigation.

Planting season: East Africa June to September. West Africa October to March.

Key pests: Onion Thrips (Thrips tabaci) -- most critical onion pest.

Key diseases: Purple Blotch, Downy Mildew.

Expected yields: 15 to 30 t/ha. Market notes: Stores 3 to 6 months if dried properly.

## 6. PLANTING SEASON CALENDAR OVERVIEW

West Africa: Long rains March to July. Short rains September to November.

East Africa: Long rains (MAM) March to May. Short rains (OND) October to December.

Southern Africa: Single rainy season November to April.

Ethiopia highlands: Main season (Meher) June to September.

South Asia: Kharif June to July (monsoon crops). Rabi October to December (winter crops).

Southeast Asia: Two to three rice crops per year with irrigation.

## 7. INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT (IPM) PRINCIPLES FOR SMALLHOLDERS

IPM combines several strategies rather than relying on pesticides alone.

Scout before you spray. Most farmers spray on a calendar basis whether needed or not. This wastes money and kills beneficial insects. Always walk through the field first, examine plants on both sides of the leaf, count pest numbers. Spray only when pest numbers are above the economic threshold.

Protect beneficial insects. Many insects in the field eat pests (ladybirds, lacewings, parasitoid wasps, ground beetles). Using selective insecticides (spinosad, Bt, neem) preserves natural enemies.

Crop rotation breaks pest and disease cycles. Example: rotate maize, then bean, then cassava, then maize.

Resistant varieties as the first line of defence. Planting a naturally resistant variety is cheaper and more effective than repeated spraying.

Biological controls. Trichogramma wasps parasitise the eggs of stem borers and bollworms. Cotesia wasps attack caterpillars from inside. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) kills caterpillars when they eat it.

Spray correctly when needed:
- Mix to the correct dosage.
- Apply at the right time -- early morning or late afternoon.
- Wear protective equipment -- gloves, face mask, long sleeves.
- Keep a record of what was sprayed, when, and at what rate.

Post-harvest pest management:
- Dry grain thoroughly before storing (below 13% moisture for maize).
- Clean storage containers before filling.
- Use hermetic bags for grain storage without chemicals.
- Inspect stored grain monthly for signs of weevils, mould, or rat damage.
- Do not store grain on the ground.

## 8. SEED SYSTEM GUIDANCE

Choosing the right seed is often the single highest-return decision a smallholder makes.

Certified Seed: Produced under quality control. Identity is guaranteed. Germination rate is tested and printed on the label. Treated seed is often pink or orange-coloured.

Open-Pollinated Varieties (OPV): A variety that breeds true -- seeds saved from an OPV crop will produce plants very similar to the parents. Farmers can save OPV seed each season. OPV seed performance declines if saved for more than two to three seasons without renewal.

F1 Hybrid Seed: Produced by crossing two specific parent lines. F1 hybrids typically yield 20 to 40% more than equivalent OPVs in favourable conditions. But seeds saved from F1 plants will NOT produce the same hybrid. Farmers using F1 hybrids must buy fresh certified seed every season.

Saved Seed (Farmer Saved Seed): Can be good quality if the farmer follows correct practices. But if saved from a hybrid, from a diseased crop, or stored poorly, quality will be poor.

Community Seed Banks: Local stores of adapted, open-pollinated varieties suited to the local climate and selected by farmers over years. These varieties may perform better than commercial varieties under stress conditions (drought, poor soil).

Signs of poor quality seed:
- Germination below 80%
- Off-type plants in the field
- Mouldy smell or visible mould
- Damaged or cracked seeds

Practical rule: If you are uncertain about seed quality, buy certified seed from a reputable agro-dealer or national seed company. The extra cost of certified seed is almost always recovered in better yields.
