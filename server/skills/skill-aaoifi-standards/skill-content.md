# AAOIFI Accounting and Sharia Standards

## What is AAOIFI?

The **Accounting and Auditing Organisation for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI)** is an international not-for-profit standard-setting organisation headquartered in Manama, Bahrain. Established in 1990, AAOIFI is the primary global body responsible for developing accounting, auditing, governance, ethics, and Sharia standards for the Islamic financial services industry.

AAOIFI has over 100 member institutions across more than 45 countries, including central banks, banking regulatory authorities, commercial and investment banks, accounting firms, and law firms. Its standards are:

- **Mandatory** in: Bahrain, Sudan, Jordan, Qatar (for Islamic banks), Syria, and several other jurisdictions.
- **Adopted by reference** (partly or wholly) in: Saudi Arabia (Saudi Central Bank encourages AAOIFI Sharia Standards), Pakistan (SBP), UAE (partially), Kuwait (partially).
- **Influential but not mandated** in: Malaysia (which uses its own Sharia Advisory Council rulings and IFRS-aligned accounting), Indonesia, and Turkey.

AAOIFI issues standards in five categories: Financial Accounting Standards (FAS), Auditing Standards for Islamic Financial Institutions (ASIFI), Governance Standards for Islamic Financial Institutions (GSIFI), Ethics Standards, and Sharia Standards (SS).

## Financial Accounting Standards (FAS)

AAOIFI Financial Accounting Standards address the specific accounting treatment of Islamic financial products and transactions, which often differ materially from IFRS treatment. Key standards include:

- **FAS 1 (General Presentation and Disclosure)** — establishes the overall framework for financial statement presentation for Islamic financial institutions. Requires separate presentation of restricted and unrestricted investment accounts.
- **FAS 7 (Investments in Associates)** — accounting for equity investments under the equity method; relevant for Musharakah structures.
- **FAS 14 (Investment Funds)** — accounting standards for Sharia-compliant investment funds.
- **FAS 23 (Consolidation)** — consolidation requirements for Islamic financial groups, including treatment of special purpose entities used in sukuk structures.
- **FAS 28 (Murabaha and Other Deferred Payment Sales)** — accounting for commodity Murabaha (cost-plus sale) financing, the most commonly used Islamic finance product. Critically differs from IFRS 9: under AAOIFI, Murabaha receivables are recognised at the transaction price (face value of the deferred sale price), not at amortised cost using the effective interest rate method. This has significant implications for financial statement presentation and capital adequacy calculations.
- **FAS 32 (Ijarah and Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek)** — accounting for Islamic leasing, including operating and finance lease variants. IFRS 16 treatment differs from FAS 32 in lessee accounting for Ijarah structures, particularly where the lessee is an Islamic bank.
- **FAS 33 (Investments in Sukuk, Shares, and Similar Instruments)** — classification and measurement of sukuk holdings. Aligns partially with IFRS 9 categories (amortised cost, fair value through OCI, fair value through P&L) but contains Islamic finance-specific adjustments.
- **FAS 35 (Risk Reserves)** — governs the accounting treatment of Profit Equalisation Reserve (PER) and Investment Risk Reserve (IRR), which are unique mechanisms used by Islamic banks to smooth returns for investment account holders (IAHs). Under PER, banks set aside a portion of profits in good years to maintain competitive returns in poor years. No IFRS equivalent exists.

**AAOIFI vs. IFRS:** The fundamental tension between AAOIFI FAS and IFRS arises because IFRS was designed for interest-based financial instruments. Islamic finance products — particularly deferred sales (Murabaha, Salam), lease-based products (Ijarah), and equity participation (Musharakah, Mudarabah) — do not fit naturally into IFRS categories. AAOIFI FAS resolve this by creating product-specific accounting standards. Institutions operating in both AAOIFI-mandatory and IFRS-reporting jurisdictions may produce dual financial statements.

## Sharia Standards (SS)

AAOIFI has published over 60 Sharia Standards covering every category of Islamic finance product and contractual arrangement. These standards are developed by AAOIFI's Sharia Board (composed of leading Islamic scholars globally) and represent authoritative rulings on Sharia permissibility and structuring requirements. Key standards:

- **SS 9 (Ijarah and Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek)** — rules for Islamic leasing, including the requirement that lessors bear ownership risk (not merely credit risk), permissibility of lease-to-own structures, and treatment of maintenance obligations.
- **SS 12 (Sharikah (Musharakah) and Modern Corporations)** — rules for equity partnership financing, including diminishing Musharakah (the dominant product for home finance), profit-sharing ratios, and loss attribution.
- **SS 17 (Investment Sukuk)** — the definitive Sharia Standard on sukuk. Specifies that sukuk must represent ownership interests in underlying tangible assets, usufructs, or services — not merely debt obligations. SS 17 is critical for sukuk structuring; non-compliance (particularly "asset-based" vs. "asset-backed" structures) is the most debated Sharia compliance issue in Islamic capital markets.
- **SS 21 (Financial Papers — Shares and Bonds)** — rules on permissibility of conventional bond holding, share ownership in companies with non-compliant activities, and purification calculations.
- **SS 23 (Agency and the Act of an Unauthorised Agent — Wakalah)** — governs agency contracts, widely used in takaful, investment management, and trade finance.
- **SS 48 (Controlling Credit Risk)** — Sharia-compliant credit risk mitigation techniques, including permissible collateral, guarantees, and netting arrangements.
- **SS 59 (Sale of Debt)** — governs permissibility and conditions for trading in debt instruments. A contentious area: AAOIFI's position differs from Malaysian practice on the permissibility of discounted debt trading (Bay al-Dayn). This divergence affects sukuk secondary market liquidity comparisons between GCC and Malaysian markets.

## Governance Standards (GSIFI)

- **GSIFI 1 (Sharia Supervisory Board — Appointment, Composition, and Report)** — sets minimum requirements for Sharia Supervisory Board (SSB) composition (minimum three scholars), appointment process, independence from management, remuneration disclosure, and the content of the annual SSB report to shareholders. This is the baseline standard for Sharia governance globally.
- **GSIFI 2 (Sharia Review)** — requirements for the internal Sharia audit and review function, separate from the SSB. Defines the scope, methodology, and reporting obligations for internal Sharia review.
- **GSIFI 6 (Statement on Governance Principles for Islamic Financial Institutions)** — overarching governance principles addressing board responsibilities, management accountability, transparency, and the rights of investment account holders (IAH), who have a quasi-equity stake but limited governance rights.

## AAOIFI vs. IFSB

These two organisations are complementary, not competing:

| Dimension | AAOIFI | IFSB |
|---|---|---|
| Headquarters | Bahrain | Kuala Lumpur |
| Focus | Product-level accounting + Sharia standards | Prudential/regulatory standards |
| Audience | Islamic banks, auditors, Sharia boards | Central banks, financial regulators |
| Equivalent to | IASB (for accounting) + Fiqh Academy (for Sharia) | Basel Committee + IAIS |
| Standard type | Product-specific (Murabaha, Ijarah, Sukuk) | Risk-based (capital adequacy, liquidity, governance) |

In practice, a full Sharia compliance assessment of an Islamic bank requires both: AAOIFI standards for product structuring and financial reporting, and IFSB standards for regulatory capital, risk management, and prudential compliance.

## AAOIFI vs. IFRS

Most GCC countries require AAOIFI FAS for Islamic banks. Malaysia uses MFRS (IFRS-based) with local Sharia Advisory Council rulings replacing AAOIFI Sharia Standards. This creates practical challenges:

- A bank operating in Bahrain (AAOIFI-mandatory) and Malaysia (IFRS/MFRS) must maintain two accounting bases.
- Sukuk listed on both Nasdaq Dubai and Bursa Malaysia may disclose under both frameworks.
- AAOIFI and IASB have initiated convergence discussions but significant divergence remains, particularly on Murabaha, Ijarah, and investment account treatment.

## Practical Application

When advising on Islamic finance compliance or structuring:

1. **First establish which jurisdiction's standards apply.** AAOIFI-mandatory jurisdictions: Bahrain, Sudan, Jordan, Qatar. AAOIFI-influential: Saudi Arabia, UAE, Pakistan. IFRS-primary: Malaysia, Indonesia, Turkey.
2. **For product Sharia compliance:** Identify the relevant AAOIFI Sharia Standard. Review SS 17 for any sukuk. Review SS 9 for Ijarah. Review SS 12 for Musharakah structures.
3. **For financial reporting:** Identify whether AAOIFI FAS or IFRS applies. Key divergence areas: Murabaha (FAS 28 vs. IFRS 9), Ijarah (FAS 32 vs. IFRS 16), risk reserves (FAS 35 — no IFRS equivalent).
4. **For Sharia governance:** Apply GSIFI 1 for SSB composition and GSIFI 2 for internal Sharia review function design.
5. **Cite standard numbers precisely.** When advising clients, referencing "AAOIFI SS 17" or "AAOIFI FAS 28" is more precise than general references to Islamic finance standards.
