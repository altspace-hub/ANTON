# Jurisdictional Skill: UAE — Central Bank of UAE (CBUAE)

You are working in the UAE financial regulatory jurisdiction. The following reference material covers the regulatory architecture — including the critical distinction between onshore UAE and the free zone jurisdictions of DIFC and ADGM — the AML/CFT framework following the country's significant post-grey-list reforms, Islamic finance, hawala regulation, and the UAE's role as a major international financial centre. Apply this context throughout your analysis. The single most important preliminary step in any UAE compliance analysis is determining whether the entity in question is onshore (CBUAE-regulated), DIFC-based (DFSA-regulated), or ADGM-based (FSRA-regulated) — the answer materially changes the applicable legal and regulatory framework.

---

## 1. Regulatory Architecture

The UAE operates a multi-layered regulatory system, with distinct authorities for onshore entities and the two major financial free zones.

**Onshore UAE:**
- **Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE):** Governs all banks (commercial, Islamic, investment), finance companies, exchange houses, payment service providers, insurance companies (jointly with the Insurance Authority), stored value facilities, and retail payment systems. CBUAE's AML/CFT supervision function has been substantially strengthened since 2022. Onshore entities are subject to UAE federal law.
- **Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA):** Regulates securities, commodities, and derivatives markets in onshore UAE. Oversees Abu Dhabi Securities Exchange (ADX), Dubai Financial Market (DFM), and licensed investment intermediaries. SCA has AML obligations aligned with CBUAE standards.
- **Insurance Authority (IA):** Regulates insurance — now being consolidated under CBUAE for supervisory purposes. Takaful (Islamic insurance) is the dominant model.

**Financial Free Zones (Separate Jurisdictions — CRITICAL):**
- **Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC):** An independent financial free zone with its own legal system based on English common law. The DIFC Courts are separate from the UAE federal courts. Regulated by the **Dubai Financial Services Authority (DFSA)** — a world-class financial regulator with comprehensive rules covering banking, capital markets, asset management, insurance, and fintech. DIFC entities are NOT subject to CBUAE regulation — they operate under DFSA rules exclusively. DIFC is home to major international banks, hedge funds, and fintech firms. When a client or counterparty has a "DIFC registered" address, DFSA rules apply, not CBUAE rules.
- **Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM):** A competing financial free zone on Al Maryah Island, Abu Dhabi. Also has English common law and its own courts. Regulated by the **Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA)**. ADGM is Abu Dhabi's international financial hub — attracting sovereign wealth fund-adjacent activities, asset management, and private equity. FSRA rules are distinct from both CBUAE and DFSA rules.
- **Other Free Zones:** Dubai Internet City, Dubai Silicon Oasis, and other economic free zones are NOT licensed financial centres — companies in these zones cannot conduct regulated financial services activities for UAE customers without separate licensing. This is a common compliance pitfall.

**Other Bodies:**
- **Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA):** Dubai-specific regulator for virtual assets. Established 2022. Governs crypto exchanges, NFT platforms, and virtual asset service providers (VASPs) operating in Dubai (excluding DIFC). VARA has issued detailed virtual asset rulebooks covering AML, market conduct, custody, and issuance.
- **UAE Financial Intelligence Unit (UAEFIU):** National FIU — operates the goAML system for STR, SAR, and CTR submissions from all reporting entities (onshore, DIFC, and ADGM). The UAEFIU is an Egmont Group member and plays a central coordination role in post-grey-list reform implementation.
- **Ministry of Economy (MoE):** Supervises DNFBPs in onshore UAE — real estate agents, gold dealers, dealers in precious metals/stones, auditors, accountants, and lawyers. MoE DNFBP registration and AML compliance has been a focus area of post-grey-list reforms.

## 2. Key Legislation

- **Federal Decree-Law No. 20/2018 on AML/CFT and Financing of Illegal Organisations:** The UAE's primary AML statute. Establishes the legal framework for criminalising money laundering and terrorist financing, defines reporting entities, and mandates CDD, ongoing monitoring, and record keeping.
- **Cabinet Decision No. 10/2019 (UBO Regulation):** Requires all onshore UAE companies to identify and register their ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) with a central registry maintained by the Ministry of Economy. UBOs are defined as individuals holding 25%+ of ownership, voting rights, or effective control.
- **CBUAE AML/CFT Standards for Licensed Financial Institutions:** The primary operational compliance framework for CBUAE-regulated entities. Covers risk-based approach, CDD, enhanced CDD, ongoing monitoring, PEP treatment, correspondent banking, wire transfers, and reporting obligations. Compliance programmes must be benchmarked against this document.
- **CBUAE Consumer Protection Regulation:** Governs customer disclosure, complaint handling, and fair treatment — relevant to compliance programme design.
- **Registered Hawala Provider (RHP) Regulation:** Requires all hawala providers to register with CBUAE. Unregistered hawala activity is a criminal offence. Licensed RHPs are subject to full AML/CFT obligations including goAML reporting.
- **DFSA Rulebook (Anti-Money Laundering, Counter-Terrorist Financing and Sanctions Module — AML):** The DFSA's comprehensive AML framework for DIFC entities. Largely aligned with FATF standards and comparable to FCA (UK) standards.
- **FSRA AML Rulebook:** ADGM's equivalent framework.

## 3. AML/CFT Framework

When conducting AML/CFT analysis for a UAE-based entity, apply the following parameters and note the post-grey-list context.

**Reporting via goAML:**
- **Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) / Suspicious Transaction Report (STR):** Mandatory. Suspicion-based — no minimum threshold. Filed promptly upon forming suspicion. Tipping off is a criminal offence.
- **Cash Transaction Report (CTR):** Required for cash transactions of AED 55,000 (~USD 15,000) or above. Given the UAE's significant cash economy (real estate, gold trading), CTR volumes are high.
- All reporting entities — onshore, DIFC, and ADGM — submit to the UAEFIU via goAML. Failure to report is a criminal offence.

**Customer Identification:**
- **UAE Nationals:** UAE National ID (Emirates ID) — mandatory biometric card issued by the Federal Authority for Identity, Citizenship, Customs and Port Security (ICP). Linked to the UAE PASS digital identity platform.
- **UAE Residents (Expats):** Emirates ID (with residency visa endorsement) — biometric, linked to passport and Emirates-wide databases. ~90% of UAE residents are expatriates; the UAE has one of the world's highest expat-to-national ratios.
- **UAE PASS:** Government-issued digital identity — enables biometric KYC for financial onboarding. CBUAE has approved UAE PASS as a valid electronic identity verification tool for account opening.
- **Company Identification:** Trade licence (issued by Dubai DED or Abu Dhabi DED or other emirate authority) + Memorandum of Association + UBO register extract. Note: free zone trade licences and mainland trade licences are different documents conferring different permissions.

**PEP Treatment:**
- UAE has a complex PEP landscape. Relevant PEP categories include: royal family members (significant wealth, global business interests), ministers and senior officials, general officers, senior executives of government-related entities (GREs — Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, Mubadala, ADNOC, DP World, Emirates NBD, etc.).
- GRE executives require particular attention — the UAE economy is substantially state-driven, and the line between private commercial activity and state-linked activity is often blurred.
- CBUAE requires all PEP relationships to receive senior management approval and enhanced ongoing monitoring.

**Beneficial Ownership:**
- Cabinet Decision No. 10/2019 mandates UBO registration for all onshore companies. The Central Beneficial Ownership Register is maintained by the Ministry of Economy. Financial institutions must verify UBO information against this register.
- Real estate sector: UAE has improved UBO disclosure for property transactions following FATF grey list — real estate registries now require UBO disclosure at purchase/sale. This was a significant gap area in the grey list assessment.

**Sanctions:**
- The UAE applies UN Security Council financial sanctions (mandatory under UAE law).
- CBUAE also maintains the UAE Local Terrorist List — designated individuals and entities under domestic law. Financial institutions must screen against both lists.
- UAE has implemented a robust sanctions screening framework post-grey-list. CBUAE conducts real-time screening expectations — verify that sanctions screening covers UN lists, CBUAE local list, and relevant jurisdiction lists (OFAC, EU, UK for internationally active entities).

## 4. FATF Grey List — Post-Exit Context

This is the single most significant recent development in UAE AML/CFT compliance. Understanding the trajectory is essential.

- **Grey List Addition (March 2022):** FATF placed the UAE on its list of jurisdictions under increased monitoring following a Mutual Evaluation Report that identified systemic weaknesses: inadequate prosecution of money laundering, real estate sector vulnerabilities, gold and precious metals sector gaps, weak beneficial ownership systems, and insufficient virtual asset regulation.
- **Reform Programme (2022-2024):** The UAE government undertook the most comprehensive AML reform programme in its history. Key measures included: establishment of VARA (virtual asset regulation), strengthened MoE DNFBP supervision, new UBO registration enforcement, real estate CDD reforms, expanded UAEFIU capacity, increased ML prosecutions and convictions, and enhanced CBUAE supervisory resources.
- **Grey List Exit (February 2024):** FATF removed the UAE from its grey list in February 2024, acknowledging substantial progress across all action plan items.

**Compliance Implications of the Post-Grey-List Environment:**

When working on UAE compliance matters, note the following:
- The supervisory environment is substantially more active than pre-2022. CBUAE examinations are more detailed, enforcement actions have increased, and institutions face genuine supervisory consequences for compliance failures.
- Correspondent banks that de-risked UAE relationships during the grey list period are gradually re-engaging, but enhanced due diligence on UAE counterparties by foreign banks remains elevated.
- DNFBPs — particularly real estate agents, gold dealers, and law firms — are under active scrutiny. If a client base or transaction set involves these sectors, apply enhanced vigilance.
- The UAE's commitment to maintaining its grey-list-exit status means regulatory expectations will remain elevated for the foreseeable future. Compliance programmes designed for the pre-2022 environment are likely inadequate.

## 5. Free Zone Distinctions — Operational Guidance

When any analysis involves a UAE entity, determine free zone vs. onshore status before applying regulatory framework.

| Entity Type | Regulatory Authority | Legal System | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onshore UAE company (LLC, PJSC) | CBUAE, SCA, MoE | UAE Federal Law / Civil Law | Can trade directly with UAE residents; most common for retail banking |
| DIFC entity | DFSA | English common law (DIFC Courts) | Premier international finance hub; English-law contracts; attracts global banks/asset managers |
| ADGM entity | FSRA | English common law (ADGM Courts) | Abu Dhabi's international hub; sovereign wealth-adjacent; growing fintech presence |
| Dubai free zone company (non-financial) | DIFC/VARA/DED depending on activity | UAE Federal Law + Free Zone rules | Cannot conduct retail banking; may need dual licence for UAE-wide operations |
| Dual licence entity | Both DFSA and CBUAE | Both | Common for banks operating in DIFC and onshore simultaneously |

A dual licence (operating in DIFC/ADGM and onshore) is common for major banks. Each licence requires a separate compliance programme aligned to the respective regulator's rules. Do not conflate DFSA requirements with CBUAE requirements in a dual-licence context.

## 6. Islamic Finance

Islamic finance in the UAE represents approximately 40-45% of banking assets, making it a significant sector requiring specialist knowledge.

- **Key Institutions:** Abu Dhabi Islamic Bank (ADIB), Dubai Islamic Bank (DIB), Emirates Islamic, Ajman Bank, Sharjah Islamic Bank.
- **Product Set:** Same core products as Saudi Arabia — Murabaha (dominant), Ijara, Musharakah/Diminishing Musharakah, Tawarruq, Sukuk. See the Saudi Arabia skill for detailed product descriptions if working across GCC jurisdictions.
- **Sharia Governance:** CBUAE has a Higher Sharia Authority (HSA) to standardise Sharia governance across UAE Islamic banks. Individual Sharia Supervisory Boards at each institution still required. CBUAE HSA fatawa (opinions) take precedence over individual SSB opinions where they conflict.
- **Sukuk Market:** Nasdaq Dubai (DIFC-based) is one of the world's largest sukuk listing venues, particularly for sovereign and quasi-sovereign sukuk from the GCC, Asia, and Africa. When working on capital markets or structured finance in the UAE context, sukuk expertise is frequently relevant.
- **AML in Islamic Finance:** Murabaha structures involving commodity exchanges require monitoring for circular transactions and structured commodity trading used as a cash flow mechanism. Tawarruq, while legitimate, has been misused in some jurisdictions — monitor for Tawarruq structures involving politically sensitive counterparties or unusual commodity flows.

## 7. Hawala Regulation

The UAE is one of the world's most significant hawala hubs, driven by large South Asian, Southeast Asian, and African diaspora populations sending remittances home.

- **Scale:** The UAE is among the top global sources of remittances. India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Philippines, and Egypt are the primary receiving corridors.
- **Regulation:** CBUAE requires all hawala service providers to register as Registered Hawala Providers (RHPs). The RHP programme includes AML/CFT obligations equivalent to exchange houses — STR reporting to UAEFIU, CDD on customers, transaction record keeping.
- **Unregistered Hawala:** A criminal offence. CBUAE actively investigates unlicensed value transfer. However, unregistered networks persist, particularly in informal trade and construction labour segments.
- **Hawaladars:** Individual hawala agents. Must be employed by or operate under a licensed RHP. Where a business appears to be operating money transfers without an RHP licence, this is a significant red flag.
- **Compliance Consideration:** Banks maintaining correspondent relationships with UAE exchange houses should conduct enhanced due diligence on the exchange house's hawala controls and RHP compliance, given the ML/TF typology risk associated with informal value transfer.

## 8. Digital Finance

- **UAE PASS:** National digital identity — enables remote onboarding, digital contract signing, and regulatory submissions. CBUAE approved for KYC use.
- **Al Etihad Payments:** The CBUAE's national payment infrastructure operator. Manages the domestic card scheme (Jaywan) and real-time payment rails.
- **UAEACH / UAE Fund Transfer System (UAEFTS):** Automated clearing house and high-value payment systems.
- **CBUAE FinTech Office and Regulatory Sandbox:** Active since 2020. Several cohorts — covering open banking, digital lending, crypto custody, and embedded finance. DIFC Innovation Testing Licence (ITL) and ADGM RegLab provide parallel sandbox environments.
- **Crypto/Virtual Assets:** A multi-regulator landscape. VARA regulates VASPs in Dubai (outside DIFC). DFSA has a crypto token framework for DIFC entities. FSRA has its own VASP framework. CBUAE has issued guidance on banks' exposure to crypto assets. The UAE is positioning Dubai as a global crypto hub — Binance, OKX, and other major exchanges have obtained VARA licences.
- **Project mBridge:** The UAE is participating in this BIS-led CBDC cross-border payments pilot alongside China, Hong Kong, Saudi Arabia, and Thailand. Relevant for cross-border payments innovation analysis.
