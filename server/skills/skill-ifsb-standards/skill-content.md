# IFSB Prudential Standards for Islamic Finance

## What is the IFSB?

The **Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB)** is an international standard-setting body headquartered in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, established in 2002 under the auspices of Bank Negara Malaysia, the IMF, and the Islamic Development Bank. The IFSB's membership comprises over 80 regulatory and supervisory authorities (central banks, financial regulators) across jurisdictions with Islamic financial services industries, as well as multilateral organisations and market participants.

The IFSB's mandate is to develop prudential standards and guiding principles for the Islamic financial services industry that complement the standards of existing international bodies — principally the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), the International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS), and the International Organisation of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) — by addressing the unique characteristics of Islamic financial products and institutions.

**Key distinction:** IFSB standards are prudential and regulatory in nature (capital, liquidity, risk management, governance). They address the supervisory relationship between regulators and Islamic financial institutions. AAOIFI standards, by contrast, address accounting, Sharia permissibility, and audit standards at the product and institutional level. Both are required for a comprehensive Islamic finance compliance framework.

## Capital Adequacy (IFSB-15, superseding IFSB-2)

**IFSB-15: Revised Capital Adequacy Standard for Institutions Offering Islamic Financial Services (IIFS)** is the primary capital adequacy standard for Islamic banks, fully superseding IFSB-2 (2005). Published in 2013 and updated to align with Basel III.

Key elements unique to Islamic banking capital adequacy:

**Investment Account Holders (IAH) and Profit-Sharing Investment Accounts (PSIA):**
Investment accounts structured as Mudarabah are not deposits in the conventional sense — depositors share in profits and, in theory, bear losses. However, in practice, Islamic banks manage "displaced commercial risk" (DCR) — the risk that depositors will withdraw funds if returns fall below market rates, creating reputational and liquidity risk. Regulators allow two approaches:
- **Full PSIAs off-balance sheet:** Regulatory capital calculated only on the bank's own funds.
- **Alpha factor (supervisory discretion):** Regulators may require Islamic banks to hold capital against a proportion of PSIA assets (alpha = 0 to 1), reflecting the degree to which displaced commercial risk materialises as institutional risk rather than investor risk.

**Other IFSB-15 Capital Considerations:**
- Equity investment risk: Capital requirements for Musharakah and Mudarabah equity exposures (different risk weights than conventional equity).
- Operational risk: Sharia non-compliance risk is explicitly classified as operational risk under IFSB-15, requiring capital attribution.
- Sukuk risk weighting: Varies by underlying asset class, not by nominal sukuk structure alone.

## Liquidity Standards (IFSB-12, IFSB-24)

Liquidity management is one of the most challenging areas for Islamic banks because conventional liquidity instruments (treasury bills, interbank deposits, overnight lending) involve interest, which is impermissible.

**IFSB-12 (Guiding Principles on Liquidity Risk Management for IIFS):**
Establishes the framework for identifying, measuring, and managing liquidity risk in Islamic banks, including:
- Liquidity risk appetite and tolerance thresholds.
- Intraday liquidity monitoring.
- Stress testing for liquidity scenarios.

**IFSB-24 (Revised Guidance Note in Connection with IFSB-12 on Liquidity Risk Management):**
Provides specific guidance on implementing Basel III liquidity standards — particularly the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) — for Islamic financial institutions. Key issues:
- **High-quality liquid assets (HQLA) for Islamic banks:** Must be Sharia-compliant. Eligible instruments include: government-issued sukuk (if sufficiently liquid), central bank Sharia-compliant instruments, and commodity Murabaha with central bank counterparties. In jurisdictions with deep sukuk markets (Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain), Islamic banks have adequate HQLA. In jurisdictions without developed Islamic sovereign debt markets, HQLA availability is constrained.
- **Lender of last resort:** Conventional central bank lending (against collateral, at interest) is unavailable to Islamic banks on Sharia grounds. IFSB-24 advocates for Sharia-compliant central bank liquidity facilities.

## Risk Management (IFSB-1)

**IFSB-1: Guiding Principles of Risk Management for Institutions Offering Islamic Financial Services** — the foundational risk management standard for Islamic banks. Addresses the full spectrum of risks inherent in Islamic finance:

- **Credit risk:** Modified for Murabaha (where the bank holds the commodity briefly), Salam (advance payment for future delivery — analogous to a commodity forward), Istisna (progressive payment for manufacturing contracts), and Ijarah (lessor bears property risk during lease term).
- **Equity investment risk:** The risk of loss on Musharakah and Mudarabah equity positions — distinct from credit risk because there is no contractual obligation to repay principal.
- **Market risk:** Commodity price risk (relevant for Murabaha and Salam structures), foreign exchange risk, and benchmark rate risk. Note: while Islamic banks do not charge interest, they typically price products off LIBOR/SOFR benchmarks as a reference rate, creating indirect benchmark rate exposure.
- **Liquidity risk:** As discussed under IFSB-12/24 above.
- **Operational risk:** Includes Sharia non-compliance risk as a distinct operational risk sub-category. A transaction that is subsequently ruled Sharia non-compliant may require purification (return of profits to charity), reputational damage, and potential regulatory sanctions. This makes Sharia audit functions a risk management imperative, not merely a governance formality.
- **Rate of return risk:** The risk that changes in market benchmark rates affect the competitive positioning of Islamic bank product returns, even though the rates themselves are not contractually adjustable in the same way as floating-rate conventional loans.

## Governance Standards (IFSB-3, IFSB-10)

**IFSB-3: Guiding Principles on Corporate Governance for Institutions Offering Islamic Financial Services:**
Addresses board composition, audit committee responsibilities, risk management oversight, disclosure, and transparency — with specific provisions for Islamic institutions, including the governance role of Sharia Supervisory Boards.

**IFSB-10: Guiding Principles on Shari'ah Governance Systems for Institutions Offering Islamic Financial Services:**
This is the IFSB's definitive governance standard for Sharia compliance frameworks:
- Minimum composition requirements for Sharia Supervisory Boards (typically three qualified scholars).
- Independence of SSB members from management and from the institution's significant shareholders.
- Fit-and-proper criteria for SSB members.
- Mandated internal Sharia compliance function (separate from the SSB) responsible for day-to-day Sharia review.
- Sharia audit function requirements.
- Disclosure requirements for SSB composition and annual report.

**IFSB-10 vs. AAOIFI GSIFI:** IFSB-10 is a prudential standard oriented towards the regulator's supervisory expectations; AAOIFI GSIFI provides more detailed operational guidance for institutions on SSB functioning and Sharia review methodologies. Most rigorous frameworks use both.

## Takaful (Islamic Insurance): IFSB-8

**IFSB-8: Guiding Principles on Governance for Takaful (Islamic Insurance) Undertakings** — addresses the unique governance, solvency, and risk management requirements for takaful operators.

Key features of takaful that differ from conventional insurance and require specific regulatory treatment:
- **Segregation of funds:** Takaful operators maintain separate participants' funds (tabarru/donation fund — the mutual insurance pool) and shareholders' funds (operator's equity). Participants' funds are not the property of the operator.
- **Wakala and Mudarabah operator models:** Different takaful models involve different fee arrangements (wakala fee on contributions vs. Mudarabah profit-sharing on investment returns). IFSB-8 addresses solvency implications of both.
- **Qard (benevolent loan):** If participants' funds are insufficient to meet claims, the takaful operator provides a Qard (interest-free loan) to the participants' fund. IFSB-8 requires operators to hold adequate reserves for potential Qard obligations.
- **Solvency margins:** Takaful-specific solvency frameworks, with guidance on adapting the IAIS Insurance Core Principles (ICPs) for takaful.
- **Retakaful:** Requirements for ceding risk to reinsurance (retakaful) counterparts using Sharia-compliant structures.

## IFSB vs. Basel Committee and IAIS

IFSB standards are explicitly designed to complement, not replace, the global standard-setter frameworks:

| Area | Conventional Standard | IFSB Complement |
|---|---|---|
| Capital adequacy (banking) | Basel III (BCBS) | IFSB-15 |
| Liquidity (banking) | Basel III LCR/NSFR | IFSB-12 + IFSB-24 |
| Risk management (banking) | BCBS Principles | IFSB-1 |
| Governance (banking) | BCBS Corporate Governance Principles | IFSB-3, IFSB-10 |
| Insurance solvency | IAIS ICPs | IFSB-8 |

Most regulators supervising Islamic financial institutions implement Basel III alongside IFSB modifications. The most sophisticated approaches issue explicit regulations specifying which Basel III provisions apply as-is, which are modified by IFSB guidance, and which are replaced by Islamic-specific frameworks.

## Practical Application

For Islamic bank capital assessment: apply **IFSB-15** for capital adequacy framework, including alpha factor determination for PSIA. For liquidity risk: apply **IFSB-12** and **IFSB-24**, assessing the quality and availability of Sharia-compliant HQLA. For risk management: apply **IFSB-1**, specifically including Sharia non-compliance risk in the operational risk framework. For Sharia governance: apply **IFSB-10**, evaluating SSB composition, independence, and internal Sharia review function design. For takaful operators: apply **IFSB-8** alongside domestic insurance solvency regulations.

For full product Sharia compliance assessment, combine IFSB standards (prudential) with the relevant **AAOIFI Sharia Standards** (product-level) for the complete Islamic finance compliance picture.
