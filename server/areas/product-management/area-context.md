# Product Management — Area Context

## Domain Overview

Product management is the discipline of discovering, defining, building, and delivering products that create value for customers and the business. It sits at the intersection of customer needs, business strategy, and technical feasibility — a triad of PM, Engineering, and Design that must function as a cohesive unit to build the right thing, the right way, at the right time.

The Product Manager (PM) and Product Owner (PO) roles are distinct but frequently confused. A PM is strategically focused: they own the "why" and "what" of the product — customer problems, market opportunities, vision, and roadmap. A PO is tactically focused: they manage the backlog, write stories, and work sprint-by-sprint with the engineering team to ensure delivery. In many organisations the roles are combined; in mature product organisations they are separate.

## Core Frameworks

**Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)** reframes product thinking around customer progress rather than features. Customers don't buy products — they hire them to get a job done. A person doesn't buy a drill; they hire it to make a hole so they can hang a shelf to feel organised. JTBD forces PMs to understand functional, social, and emotional motivations before designing solutions, and underpins techniques like opportunity solution trees and continuous discovery.

**OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)** connect product work to business outcomes. An Objective is qualitative and inspirational ("Make onboarding feel effortless"). Key Results are measurable and time-bound ("Reduce time to first value from 14 days to 3 days by Q3"). OKRs are the translation layer between company strategy and product roadmaps, and force teams to commit to outcomes rather than output lists.

**RICE Prioritisation** (Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort) is a structured framework for scoring backlog items. Reach estimates how many users are affected per period; Impact scores the expected effect on the target metric; Confidence reflects certainty in the estimates; Effort is estimated person-months. The resulting RICE score allows objective comparison across items of very different natures.

**The Kano Model** classifies features into three types: must-have (threshold) features whose absence causes dissatisfaction but whose presence is invisible; performance features where more is always better; and delighters (excitement features) that surprise and engage users. Kano prevents teams from over-investing in features users expect by default while under-investing in differentiators.

## Agile and Scrum Context

Modern product management operates within agile delivery frameworks. Scrum structures work into two-week sprints with ceremonies: sprint planning, daily standups, sprint review, and retrospective. The product backlog — a prioritised list of user stories and tasks — is the PM/PO's primary artefact. Kanban offers a flow-based alternative without fixed sprint cadences, better suited to support, operations, and continuous delivery contexts.

## Product Lifecycle Stages

Products move through predictable stages: Discovery (validating the problem and solution fit before building), 0-to-1 (building the minimum viable product and finding early adopters), Growth (scaling what works), Maturity (defending market position and extending lifetime value), and Revival (repositioning or transforming a declining product). Strategy, metrics, team structure, and risk appetite all change fundamentally across these stages.

## Product-Led Growth

The shift to product-led growth (PLG) is one of the most significant structural changes in modern software. PLG companies — Slack, Figma, Notion — acquire and expand users through the product itself rather than through sales and marketing. Free trials, freemium tiers, viral loops, and in-product activation are the growth engine. This shifts the PM's mandate: activation metrics, time-to-value, and in-product conversion funnels become as important as feature delivery. PLG does not eliminate the need for sales; it changes when sales engages (after the product proves value, not before).
