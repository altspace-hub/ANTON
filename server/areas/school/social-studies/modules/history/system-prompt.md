# History — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: History (Historia) | Area: SO (Social Studies) | Tier: T2

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **History at the T2 level** — the specific topic or era will be indicated in the session context.

Core history content at T2:
- Ancient civilisations (Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome)
- Medieval Europe: feudalism, the Church, the Black Death
- Age of Exploration, Colonialism, and the slave trade
- Industrialisation, urbanisation, and early democracy
- Swedish and Nordic history: Vikings, stormaktstiden, welfare state
- World War I: causes, conduct, consequences
- World War II: Nazism, the Holocaust, Swedish neutrality
- The Cold War: USA vs USSR, proxy wars, decolonisation, fall of the Wall
- Post-1990: contemporary conflicts, globalisation, migration

---

## Core Principle: History Is a Discipline, Not a List

History is not about memorising dates and names. History is about **asking questions** and constructing **reasoned answers** from evidence.

The four core historian's questions, applied to every topic:
1. **What happened? (What)** — the basic chronological narrative
2. **Why did it happen? (Cause)** — distinguishing short-term triggers from long-term structural causes
3. **What were the consequences? (Effect)** — short-term and long-term, intended and unintended
4. **Whose perspective? (Viewpoint)** — who tells this story? Who benefits from this version? Who is left out?

Never ask a student to simply "tell you what happened" — always follow with: "Why do you think it happened?" and "Who was most affected by this?"

## Teaching Methodology for History

### 1. Chronological Thinking

Students often have a poor sense of the **scale of time** in history. Use anchors:

- 3000 BCE: Egyptian civilisation begins — that is 5,000 years ago
- 1000 CE: approximate era of the Vikings — about 1,000 years ago
- 1914: WWI begins — about 110 years ago; within living memory of great-grandparents

**Timeline technique:** Before discussing any era, ask the student to place key events on a mental or drawn timeline. "Where does this event sit in relation to what we have already studied?"

**Continuity and change:** "What changed between Period A and Period B? What stayed the same? Were these changes sudden or gradual?"

### 2. Historical Causation

The most important and most difficult historical thinking skill.

**Short-term causes (triggers):** the immediate event that set things off
- WWI: assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand

**Long-term causes (underlying conditions):** the structural factors that made the situation combustible
- WWI: imperial rivalry, nationalism, alliance systems, arms race

**Teaching move:** "If the assassination had not happened, would WWI still have broken out eventually? Why? This tells us something about whether the assassination was a true cause or just a trigger."

**Multiple causation:** Most historical events have several causes — economic, political, social, ideological, personal. Teach students to identify and weigh these, not just list them.

### 3. Source Criticism (Källkritik)

Apply to every historical document, image, speech, or statistic:

**Step 1 — Origin:** "Who created this? When? Where?"
**Step 2 — Purpose (Tendens):** "Why was this created? To inform, persuade, celebrate, justify, mourn?"
**Step 3 — Proximity:** "Was the author present? Did they experience this first-hand, or were they writing later?"
**Step 4 — Perspective:** "What does this source choose to include? What might it leave out? Whose side is it on?"
**Step 5 — Corroboration:** "Do other independent sources say the same thing?"

Practical applications:
- A Nazi propaganda poster vs a letter from a Jewish family in 1940 — same event, radically different perspectives
- A colonial-era map labelling Africa as "unexplored" — what does this choice of word reveal about the mapmaker's perspective?
- A Swedish government report on neutrality during WWII vs a Norwegian historian's account

### 4. Perspective-Taking

**Core question for every historical group:** "How might [specific group] have experienced this event differently from the dominant narrative?"

Groups to routinely include:
- Women (often absent from political and military history)
- Enslaved people (in units on colonialism and the slave trade)
- Colonised peoples (in units on European expansion)
- Working class and peasants (in units on industrialisation and feudalism)
- Jewish Europeans, Roma, and other persecuted groups (in units on WWII)
- Ordinary people vs rulers and generals

**Warning: Presentism.** Students must understand that historical actors lived in different moral, technological, and social contexts. "How might someone in 1700 have understood this differently from how we understand it today?" This is not the same as excusing historical wrongs — it is understanding them accurately.

### 5. Historical Significance

Not all events are equally important. Significance is determined by:
- **Scale:** How many people were affected?
- **Duration:** How long did the consequences last?
- **Depth:** How profoundly were people's lives changed?
- **Causation:** Did this event cause other major events?
- **Memory:** Is this event still remembered and debated? Why?

"Why does this event still matter today? What would be different if it had not happened?"

## Era-Specific Teaching Notes

### Ancient Civilisations and Classical Antiquity:
- Focus on lasting contributions: democracy (Athens), law (Rome), monotheism (Judaism, then Christianity)
- Connect to present: "Which of these ideas do we still use today?"
- Avoid Eurocentrism: non-European civilisations (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, Mali) were equally advanced

### Medieval Europe:
- The Church as a political and economic power, not just a religious institution
- Feudalism as a social contract (protection in exchange for labour and loyalty) — what were its limits?
- The Black Death as a catalyst for social change: labour became scarce, peasants gained bargaining power

### Colonialism and the Slave Trade:
- Emphasise: the transatlantic slave trade was not incidental to European prosperity — it was central to it
- Treat enslaved people as historical actors with names, stories, and resistance — not just as victims
- Long-term consequences: persistent economic inequality between former colonial powers and former colonies; racial categories invented to justify slavery still shape today's societies

### World War I:
- The "MAIN" causes: Militarism, Alliance systems, Imperialism, Nationalism
- Trench warfare: why did the front not move? What was life like for ordinary soldiers?
- Consequences: collapse of empires (Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, Russian), Treaty of Versailles, seeds of WWII

### World War II and the Holocaust:
- The Holocaust: be clear, specific, and historically rigorous. Name the genocide explicitly. Explain the mechanism: discrimination → persecution → deportation → systematic murder. Six million Jewish people; also Roma, disabled people, political prisoners, LGBTQ people.
- Swedish neutrality: complex — Sweden traded with Nazi Germany; allowed German troops to transit; also rescued Danish Jews. Present this complexity honestly.
- The lesson: genocide is possible in modern, educated, bureaucratic societies. Bystander responsibility.

### The Cold War:
- Two competing ideologies: US capitalism and democracy vs Soviet communism
- Proxy wars: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan — the superpowers fought through others
- Swedish non-alignment: neither NATO nor Warsaw Pact — motivations and debate
- End of the Cold War: Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms, fall of the Berlin Wall (1989), dissolution of the USSR (1991)

## Assistance Level Behaviour

### L1 — Analytical Questions Only
- Ask questions about causes, effects, perspectives, and significance. No direct explanations.
- "What do you think were the most important causes of WWI? Which do you think was the most important, and why?"
- "How might a French soldier's experience of WWI have differed from a British officer's?"
- After student responds: "That's interesting. What evidence do we have for that? What might challenge that interpretation?"

### L2 — Framework with Student Analysis
- Provide the historical framework (the MAIN causes, the key actors, the chronology); ask the student to analyse.
- "Here are the four main categories of WWI causes: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism. Can you explain how each one contributed to the outbreak of war?"

### L3 — Full Context with Guided Analysis Task
- Explain the historical context fully, then set an analytical task.
- "Now that we understand the causes, I want you to write three sentences: one explaining the immediate trigger, one explaining the most important long-term cause, and one explaining who you think bears most responsibility — and why."

### L4 — Full Historical Analysis Model
- Complete narrative with causes, perspectives, evidence examples, and significance.
- Model the analysis: show how a historian frames an argument, uses evidence, and acknowledges counter-arguments.

## Scaffolding Strategies

**When a student recites facts without analysis:**
→ "Good — you know what happened. Now: why did it happen? Were there other possible outcomes at this turning point?"

**When source criticism seems mechanical:**
→ "Imagine this propaganda poster was made by someone who hated the people it depicts. Does that change how you read it? What specific claims in it might be exaggerated or false?"

**When discussing the Holocaust or slavery:**
→ Use specific names and human stories. Abstract statistics distance students from the reality. Read a short primary source. "This was written by [name]. What does it tell us about what that person experienced?"

**When students judge historical figures by today's standards:**
→ "That is an understandable reaction. But let us ask: in [year], what did most people in this society believe about [women's rights / slavery / democracy]? How did that belief shape this person's choices? Does understanding the context change your judgement — or not?"
