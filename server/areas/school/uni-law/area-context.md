# Introduction to Law — University Level

## Subject Overview
University law education introduces students to the nature of legal systems, the sources and
hierarchy of law, legal reasoning and interpretation, and the major substantive areas including
contract, tort, criminal, constitutional, and public law. Students develop skills in case analysis,
statutory interpretation, legal argument, and academic legal writing. The civil law and common law
traditions are both represented.

## Core Areas

### Legal Systems & Sources of Law
- Common law vs. civil law traditions; mixed legal systems
- Sources of law: legislation, case law (precedent/stare decisis), custom, equity
- Hierarchy of norms: constitutional law, statute, delegated legislation, case law
- International law: treaties, customary international law, jus cogens
- European law (EU): supremacy, direct effect, preliminary rulings (Article 267 TFEU)

### Legal Reasoning & Interpretation
- The ratio decidendi and obiter dicta; distinguishing and overruling precedent
- Statutory interpretation: literal, golden, mischief rules; purposive interpretation
- ECHR / fundamental rights as interpretive tools
- Legal syllogism: IRAC method (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion)
- Inductive vs. deductive reasoning in legal argument

### Contract Law
- Formation: offer, acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations
- Terms: express, implied (by statute, custom, courts); conditions vs. warranties
- Vitiating factors: misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, mistake
- Breach and remedies: damages (expectation, reliance), specific performance, rescission
- Consumer contracts and unfair terms (EU Directive 93/13; UK UCTA 1977)

### Tort Law
- Negligence: duty of care (Caparo test), breach, causation (but-for; Bonnington Castings), damage
- Defamation: libel vs. slander; defences; Reynolds privilege
- Occupiers' liability; nuisance (private and public); trespass
- Strict liability: Rylands v. Fletcher; product liability (EU Directive 85/374)

### Constitutional & Administrative Law
- Constitutional principles: separation of powers, rule of law, parliamentary sovereignty
- Judicial review: illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety (GCHQ case)
- Human rights: ECHR Articles 5, 6, 8, 10, 14; margin of appreciation
- EU constitutional architecture: institutions, competences, subsidiarity, proportionality

### Criminal Law
- Actus reus and mens rea; strict liability offences
- Inchoate offences: attempt, conspiracy, incitement
- General defences: consent, necessity, self-defence, duress
- Major offences: homicide (murder, manslaughter), theft, fraud, sexual offences

## Pedagogical Approach
Professor Lindström uses the case-based Socratic method:
1. **Case first** — read the facts and judgement before discussing doctrine
2. **IRAC discipline** — structure every legal problem with Issue-Rule-Application-Conclusion
3. **Counter-argument** — always present the strongest opposing legal argument
4. **Policy awareness** — consider why the law developed this way and what values it serves
5. **Jurisdiction awareness** — specify which legal system is being discussed at all times

## Academic Standards
- Cite cases in full (Donoghue v. Stevenson [1932] AC 562) and statutes with section numbers
- Distinguish between holding, reasoning, and dicta in case analysis
- Academic legal writing: footnotes, OSCOLA citation style
- No practitioner advice — academic legal analysis only
