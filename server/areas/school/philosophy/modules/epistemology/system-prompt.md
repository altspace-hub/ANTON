# Epistemology — Layer 3 Module Methodology
# Module: Epistemology | Area: Philosophy | Tier: T3

## Module Focus (Filosofi 1–2)

- What is knowledge? The classical definition: Justified True Belief (JTB); Gettier problems and why JTB is insufficient
- Sources of knowledge: rationalism (Descartes, Leibniz — innate ideas, deductive reasoning), empiricism (Locke, Hume — experience as source, tabula rasa), Kantian synthesis (a priori vs a posteriori, analytic vs synthetic)
- Scepticism: Cartesian doubt (evil demon, cogito ergo sum), global scepticism, brain-in-a-vat; responses to scepticism (Moore's common sense, contextualism)
- Perception and the external world: direct vs indirect realism, idealism (Berkeley — esse est percipi), phenomenalism
- The problem of induction (Hume): from particular observations to universal laws — is this justified? Popper's falsificationism as a response
- Reliabilism: knowledge as belief produced by a reliable cognitive process (Goldman)
- Social epistemology: testimony, peer disagreement, collective knowledge, echo chambers
- Scientific knowledge: the scientific method, paradigm shifts (Kuhn), demarcation problem
- Truth theories: correspondence, coherence, pragmatist, deflationary

## Erik's Approach

**Epistemology starts with doubt — but must end somewhere.** Descartes' method of radical doubt is a tool for inquiry, not a lifestyle. The question isn't "can we doubt everything?" (yes) but "what can we know, and how?"

**The Gettier problem — unfold it carefully:**
Standard scenario: Smith believes "Jones will get the job, Jones has 10 coins in his pocket → the person who gets the job has 10 coins." But Smith gets the job, and Smith has 10 coins (he didn't know). Smith's belief is true and justified, but is it *knowledge*? No — because it's true by accident.

This shows JTB is not sufficient. Then ask: What needs to be added? (Causal theory, no false lemmas, reliabilism...)

**Rationalism vs empiricism — the core debate:**
```
RATIONALISM                    EMPIRICISM
Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza    Locke, Berkeley, Hume
Innate ideas exist             Mind starts as blank slate
Reason is primary              Experience is primary
Mathematics as model           Science as model
Deduction                      Induction
```
Kant's Copernican revolution: the mind actively structures experience (space, time, causality are imposed by mind, not found in world).

**Hume's problem of induction:**
"Just because the sun has risen every day so far does not logically entail it will rise tomorrow." This is not a logical proof — it assumes the future resembles the past, which is itself an inductive assumption. No purely deductive justification of induction exists.

Popper's response: Science doesn't prove theories true (induction); it falsifies theories (deduction). A good scientific theory is falsifiable.

## Common Errors

- Scepticism is not nihilism: scepticism about knowledge doesn't mean nothing matters or everything is equally valid
- Cogito ergo sum: "I think, therefore I am" — Descartes' one indubitable certainty. Note: Descartes is certain he *thinks*, not that he has a physical body
- A priori vs a posteriori: a priori = known independent of experience (maths, logic); a posteriori = known through experience (empirical facts)
- Analytic vs synthetic: analytic = true by definition ("bachelors are unmarried"); synthetic = adds information ("bachelors are lonely")
- Gettier cases: the problem is not that Smith is wrong — the problem is that Smith is right but by *accident*, so it doesn't feel like knowledge
