# Logic — Layer 3 Module Methodology
# Module: Logic | Area: Philosophy | Tier: T3

## Module Focus (Filosofi 1–2)

- Argument structure: premises, conclusion, inference; distinguishing arguments from explanations, reports, and descriptions
- Validity and soundness: valid = if premises true, conclusion must be true; sound = valid + premises actually true
- Deductive reasoning: modus ponens (P→Q, P ∴ Q), modus tollens (P→Q, ¬Q ∴ ¬P), hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism
- Inductive reasoning: generalisation, analogy, statistical inference; strength vs weakness; the problem of induction
- Abductive reasoning: inference to the best explanation; criteria for best explanation (simplicity, scope, fit)
- Propositional logic: propositions (P, Q, R), connectives (¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔), truth tables, tautologies, contradictions
- Predicate logic (introduction): quantifiers (∀ = "for all", ∃ = "there exists"), predicates, translating natural language to formal notation
- Informal fallacies: ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, appeal to authority, appeal to ignorance, begging the question (circular reasoning), hasty generalisation, post hoc ergo propter hoc, red herring, tu quoque
- Argument analysis: reconstructing arguments, identifying implicit premises, evaluating argument quality
- Scientific reasoning: hypothesis testing, null hypothesis, correlation vs causation, confirmation bias

## Erik's Approach

**Logic is the grammar of good thinking.** Just as bad grammar makes communication unclear, bad logic makes reasoning unreliable. These are not academic exercises — they are survival skills for navigating information.

**The three Cs of argument evaluation:**
1. **Clear:** Do I understand what is being claimed? (Define key terms)
2. **Valid:** If the premises were true, would the conclusion follow? (Logical structure)
3. **Sound:** Are the premises actually true? (Empirical check)

**Modus ponens and modus tollens — drill these:**
- MP: If it rains, the ground gets wet. It is raining. ∴ The ground is wet. ✓ Valid
- MT: If it rains, the ground gets wet. The ground is NOT wet. ∴ It is not raining. ✓ Valid
- Affirming the consequent (INVALID): If it rains, the ground gets wet. The ground IS wet. ∴ It rained. ✗ (Could be a sprinkler!)
- Denying the antecedent (INVALID): If it rains, the ground gets wet. It is NOT raining. ∴ The ground is not wet. ✗

**Truth table for key connectives:**
| P | Q | P∧Q | P∨Q | P→Q | ¬P |
|---|---|-----|-----|-----|----|
| T | T |  T  |  T  |  T  |  F |
| T | F |  F  |  T  |  F  |  F |
| F | T |  F  |  T  |  T  |  T |
| F | F |  F  |  F  |  T  |  T |

**Fallacy detection as everyday skill:** When reading a news article or debate, identify claims, spot premises, look for fallacies. Common ones in political discourse:
- Straw man: misrepresenting opponent's position to attack an easier target
- False dilemma: "You're either with us or against us" (ignores middle ground)
- Post hoc: "We introduced policy X → crime fell → X reduced crime" (correlation ≠ causation)

## Common Errors

- Validity ≠ truth: "All fish fly. Erik is a fish. Erik flies" is VALID (if premises true, conclusion follows) but unsound (premises are false). Students must separate these concepts
- P→Q does not mean Q→P (converse). "If it rains, ground is wet" does NOT imply "If ground is wet, it rained"
- Ad hominem: attacking the person, not the argument. "X said Y but X is corrupt" — this may be relevant to credibility but does NOT refute Y. The argument must still be addressed on its merits
- Inductive arguments cannot be "valid" or "invalid" — they are strong or weak (valid/invalid are deductive terms)
- Correlation vs causation: this is not just an academic point — confusing them drives bad policy decisions every day
