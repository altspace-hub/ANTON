# Module: Political Philosophy | Area: Philosophy | Tier: T3

## Module Focus (Filosofi 1–2)

- State legitimacy: what justifies political authority? Consent theories (Locke, Rousseau), natural duty, associative obligations
- Social contract theories: Hobbes (state of nature as war, Leviathan — absolute sovereignty for security), Locke (natural rights, limited government, right to revolt), Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty, civil liberty)
- Justice and distribution: Rawls (justice as fairness, difference principle, primary goods), Nozick (entitlement theory, historical justice, minimal state, taxation as forced labour), Walzer (complex equality, separate spheres)
- Liberty: negative liberty (Berlin — freedom from interference), positive liberty (freedom to, capabilities), libertarianism, republicanism (non-domination — Pettit)
- Democracy: direct vs representative democracy, deliberative democracy (Habermas — communicative rationality), democratic legitimacy, majority tyranny vs minority rights
- Rights: natural rights, human rights, legal rights; rights as trumps (Dworkin); universalism vs cultural relativism in human rights
- Power and authority: Weber's types of authority (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational); Foucault on power/knowledge
- Political ideologies: liberalism, conservatism, socialism/Marxism, feminism, environmentalism — philosophical foundations
- Global justice: cosmopolitanism (Singer, Pogge — obligations to global poor), nationalism, distributive justice beyond borders
- Civil disobedience: Thoreau, MLK — when is it justified? Requirements (open, nonviolent, accept punishment, last resort)

## Erik's Approach

**Political philosophy asks the foundational question: why should anyone obey anyone else?** This is not abstract — it is the question behind every law, institution, and election. Start here, then build.

**The state of nature thought experiment:**
All three contractarians use it differently:
- **Hobbes:** State of nature = "war of all against all" (nasty, brutish, short). Solution: give up all rights to a sovereign in exchange for security. Sovereignty is near-absolute.
- **Locke:** State of nature = mostly peaceful (natural law governs), but insecure property. Solution: limited government to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property). Government can be overthrown if it violates these.
- **Rousseau:** State of nature = happy, corruption from society. Solution: the general will — a democratic collective that expresses true freedom (not individual desires but common good).

**Rawls vs Nozick — the central justice debate:**
| | Rawls | Nozick |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Veil of ignorance | Historical entitlement |
| Key principle | Difference principle | Non-aggression principle |
| On redistribution | Justified if it helps worst-off | Unjust — violates rights |
| State role | Moderate welfare state | Minimal state only |
| Objection received | Ignores individual choices | Ignores structural injustice |

**Berlin's two concepts of liberty:**
- **Negative:** "Freedom from" — no one is preventing you. No interference. Valued by liberals and libertarians.
- **Positive:** "Freedom to" — capacity and power to act. Requires resources, education, health. Valued by social democrats.
The distinction matters: a starving person is "free" in the negative sense but not in the positive sense.

**Civil disobedience — when justified (Rawls' criteria):**
1. Serious injustice being protested (not mere policy disagreement)
2. Legal channels exhausted
3. Action is open and nonviolent
4. Willingness to accept legal consequences (shows respect for rule of law overall)

## Common Errors

- Hobbes is not a fascist: the Leviathan is a social contract — sovereignty is granted by the people for mutual protection, not imposed by conquest
- Locke's "property" includes life and liberty — it is not just material possessions. Violation of any natural right justifies resistance
- Rawls' difference principle: inequalities are permitted if (and only if) they benefit the least advantaged. Not "the poor deserve something" but "structure institutions so they work for the most vulnerable"
- Nozick: he accepts that a minimal state is justified (police, courts, enforcement of contracts) — he is not an anarchist
- Negative liberty: "absence of interference" does NOT mean "whatever you want is fine." Negative liberty is bounded by others' equal liberty
- Civil disobedience ≠ revolution: civil disobedience accepts the legitimacy of the legal system overall; revolution rejects it
