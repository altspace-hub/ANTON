# Evolution — Layer 3 Module Methodology
# Module: Evolution | Area: Biology | Tier: T3

## Module Focus (Biologi 1–2)

- Darwin's theory: natural selection, variation, heritability, differential reproduction, adaptation
- Evidence for evolution: fossil record, comparative anatomy (homologous/analogous structures, vestigial organs), biogeography, molecular evidence (DNA similarity, cytochrome c)
- Mechanisms of evolution: mutation (ultimate source of variation), genetic drift (founder effect, bottleneck effect), gene flow, natural selection, sexual selection
- Types of natural selection: directional, stabilising, disruptive selection (with frequency distribution diagrams)
- Speciation: allopatric (geographic isolation → reproductive isolation → new species), sympatric; reproductive isolation mechanisms (pre- vs post-zygotic)
- Hardy-Weinberg revisited: evolution defined as change in allele frequencies; H-W equilibrium = no evolution
- Human evolution: Australopithecus → Homo habilis → H. erectus → H. sapiens; key features (bipedalism, brain size, tool use)
- Macroevolution: mass extinctions (5 major), adaptive radiation, convergent evolution, co-evolution
- Classification (phylogenetics): Linnaean hierarchy (Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species); binomial nomenclature; cladistics and cladograms

## Viktor's Approach

**Evolution is not a plan — it is a filter.** Natural selection does not create variation; it acts on existing variation. Only heritable variation that affects survival/reproduction drives evolution. Frame every question: "What is the selection pressure? What variation exists? What is the outcome over generations?"

**Common misconception head-on:** "Species evolve to survive" is teleological (implies purpose). The correct framing: "Individuals with traits better suited to current conditions leave more offspring → those traits increase in frequency." Evolution has no foresight.

**Natural selection diagram:**
```
Original population (variation exists)
↓ Selection pressure (predation, drought, disease)
↓ Differential survival/reproduction
↓ Next generation — higher frequency of favourable alleles
↓ Over many generations → adaptation
```

**Speciation requires reproductive isolation.** Geographic barriers (allopatric) are clearest. Once populations no longer interbreed, genetic divergence accumulates.

**Cladograms:** Read from branch points (nodes = common ancestors). More shared nodes = more closely related. Humans and chimpanzees share a more recent common ancestor than humans and gorillas.

**Evidence hierarchy:**
1. Molecular evidence (most powerful — DNA sequences are quantifiable)
2. Fossil record (direct but incomplete)
3. Comparative anatomy and biogeography (support, pattern evidence)

## Common Errors

- "Survival of the fittest" — "fittest" means best suited to current environment, not strongest or fastest
- Lamarckian inheritance: acquired characteristics are NOT inherited (giraffes did not stretch necks and pass length to offspring). Variation must be heritable (genetic) to drive evolution
- Genetic drift vs natural selection: drift is random; selection is non-random (consistently favours better-adapted variants)
- Analogous structures (wings of bats vs insects): similar function, different ancestry = convergent evolution ≠ evidence of common ancestor. Homologous structures (arm bones across vertebrates) = common ancestry
- Speciation: two populations becoming genetically different is NOT speciation — speciation requires reproductive isolation (they cannot interbreed even if they come back into contact)
- Evolution applies to POPULATIONS, not individuals. An individual cannot evolve during its lifetime
