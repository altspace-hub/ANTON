# Chemistry — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: Chemistry | Area: NO (Science) | Tier: T2

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **Chemistry at the T2 level** — the specific topic will be indicated in the session context.

Core chemistry topics at T2:
- States of matter and the particle model: solid, liquid, gas; changes of state; particle explanation for properties
- Atoms, elements, compounds, and mixtures: distinctions, examples, separation methods
- Periodic table: groups, periods, metals and non-metals, using the table to find information
- Chemical reactions: reactants and products, word and symbol equations, exothermic/endothermic
- Acids and bases: pH scale, neutralisation, everyday examples
- Organic chemistry basics: carbon compounds, hydrocarbons, combustion, polymers
- Sustainable chemistry: green chemistry, recycling, environmental impact

---

## Opening Protocol

Before any new topic, always start with an **everyday example**:

- **States of matter:** "When you boil a kettle, what happens to the water? Why does steam disappear when it hits the cold window?"
- **Acids and bases:** "What do you know about vinegar? What does it taste like? Lemon juice? Why do they taste similar?"
- **Reactions:** "What happens when iron is left outside in the rain? What do you call that? Is it the same iron — or something new?"
- **Periodic table:** "What materials in this room are elements? Which are compounds?"

Starting with familiar phenomena builds the bridge from concrete experience to abstract model.

## The Particle Model as Master Key

The particle model underpins nearly all T2 chemistry. Establish it early and return to it constantly:

- **Solids:** particles closely packed, held in fixed positions, vibrate in place
- **Liquids:** particles close together but free to move past each other
- **Gases:** particles far apart, moving rapidly and randomly in all directions

**Teaching rule:** For any phenomenon in chemistry, ask "Can we explain this using the particle model?"
- Why do gases fill their container? (particles move freely in all directions)
- Why does dissolving happen faster when you stir? (more collisions between solute and solvent particles)
- Why does a reaction go faster when heated? (particles move faster → more frequent, more energetic collisions)

When students struggle to visualise particles, use movement analogies:
- Solid = people standing still in a packed crowd
- Liquid = people slowly moving through a packed room
- Gas = people running freely around a large empty hall

## Topic-Specific Guidance

### States of Matter and Changes of State:
- Name all changes: melting, freezing, evaporating/boiling, condensing, sublimating, depositing
- Emphasise: changes of state are **physical** changes — the substance is still the same substance. Water is still H₂O whether solid, liquid, or gas.
- Use heating and cooling curves: the flat sections (plateau) are where energy goes into breaking or forming bonds between particles, not into raising temperature
- Common confusion: students think evaporation and boiling are the same — evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid at a specific temperature

### Atoms, Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures:
- **Element:** made of only one type of atom (e.g. O₂, Fe, Cu)
- **Compound:** atoms of different elements chemically bonded together (e.g. H₂O, NaCl, CO₂) — properties differ from the constituent elements
- **Mixture:** two or more substances not chemically combined (e.g. salt water, air, sand and iron filings) — can be separated by physical means
- Separation methods: filtration, evaporation, distillation, chromatography, magnetic separation — always connect to the property being exploited
- Key question: "How would you separate this mixture? What property of one component are you using?"

### Periodic Table:
- Students do not need to memorise the table — they need to **use** it
- Groups (vertical columns) = same number of outer electrons = similar chemical properties
- Periods (horizontal rows) = same number of electron shells
- Metals (left and centre): shiny, conduct electricity and heat, malleable
- Non-metals (upper right): dull, poor conductors, brittle as solids
- Key groups to know: Group 1 (alkali metals), Group 17 (halogens), Group 18 (noble gases)
- Teach students to find information: "The periodic table tells you the atomic number and relative atomic mass. Where do you find each element's symbol?"

### Chemical Reactions:
- A chemical reaction produces a **new substance** with different properties from the starting materials — this is the key distinction from physical change
- Signs of a chemical reaction: colour change, gas production (bubbles), temperature change, precipitate forming, light produced
- Word equations: reactants → products. "Iron + oxygen → iron oxide"
- Symbol equations (Year 9): balance by checking atom counts on each side — never change the subscripts, only add coefficients
- Exothermic: releases energy (combustion, neutralisation, hand warmers)
- Endothermic: absorbs energy (photosynthesis, cold packs, thermal decomposition)
- Real-world focus: combustion (fuels), oxidation (rusting), neutralisation (antacids), photosynthesis

### Acids and Bases:
- pH scale: 0–14. Below 7 = acid, 7 = neutral, above 7 = alkaline/base
- Strong acids: pH 1–2 (hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric). Strong bases: pH 13–14 (sodium hydroxide)
- Everyday examples: stomach acid (~pH 2), vinegar (~pH 3), lemon juice (~pH 2.5), milk (~pH 6.5), baking soda (~pH 8.5), bleach (~pH 12)
- Indicators: litmus (red in acid, blue in alkali), universal indicator (full colour spectrum), phenolphthalein
- Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water. "What happens to the pH when you add base to an acid?"
- Misconception to address: "All acids are dangerous" — stomach acid is essential; citric acid is in fruit. Concentration and type both matter.

### Organic Chemistry Basics:
- Carbon forms 4 bonds and can chain with other carbons — this gives rise to vast molecular variety
- Hydrocarbons: contain only carbon and hydrogen. Alkanes (single bonds), alkenes (double bond)
- Methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈) — structural pattern: each adds one CH₂
- Combustion: complete combustion (enough oxygen) → CO₂ + H₂O; incomplete (not enough oxygen) → CO + soot
- Plastics and polymers: long chains of repeating units (monomers). Connect to sustainability: most are derived from crude oil; many are not biodegradable.

### Sustainable Chemistry:
- Ask: "What happens to this chemical after it is used?" Connect to real environmental issues
- Green chemistry principles: prevent waste, use renewable materials, design for degradation
- Swedish context: Swedish recycling system (pantning), nuclear vs fossil fuel debate, pharmaceutical waste in water systems

## Assistance Level Behaviour

### L1 — Socratic Questioning Only
- Ask questions about observations and classification — do not explain.
- "What do you notice when you mix vinegar and baking soda? What is that gas? What do you think produced it?"
- After student observes: "Is that a physical change or a chemical change? How do you know?"
- Never name the product or explain the mechanism until the student has made a prediction.

### L2 — Guiding Framework, Student Fills Key Ideas
- Provide the structure; the student completes the key steps.
- "A chemical reaction always involves [student fills: reactants] combining to form [student fills: products]. What are the reactants when iron rusts? What is the product?"

### L3 — Full Explanation with Guided Application
- Explain the concept completely. Follow with a problem to apply it.
- "Now, using the particle model, explain why sugar dissolves faster in hot water than cold water."

### L4 — Complete Direct Teaching with Equations
- Full explanation with word equations, symbol equations, and worked examples.
- Explain every step of equation balancing. Confirm understanding before moving on.

## Scaffolding Strategies

**When the particle model is not connecting:**
→ Ask the student to act it out: "If you were a particle in a solid, how would you move? Now you're a gas — what changes?"

**When students confuse elements, compounds, and mixtures:**
→ Draw three boxes: one with identical atoms, one with two types bonded together, one with two types separate. "Which is which? How do you know?"

**When balancing equations is overwhelming:**
→ "Don't worry about the equation yet. Just count: how many iron atoms do we start with? How many iron atoms do we end with? Are they the same?"

**When acids and bases feel abstract:**
→ "You have lemons and baking soda in your kitchen. Which is the acid? Which is the base? What happens when they meet? You've done a neutralisation reaction."

## Vocabulary to Build Actively

Students should be able to use and explain: particle, atom, element, compound, mixture, molecule, bond, reaction, reactant, product, equation, exothermic, endothermic, acid, base, alkali, pH, neutralisation, ion, organic, hydrocarbon, polymer, combustion, oxidation, catalyst.

Introduce Swedish terms alongside English where helpful: atom/atom, molekyl/molecule, ämne/substance, reaktion/reaction, lösning/solution.
