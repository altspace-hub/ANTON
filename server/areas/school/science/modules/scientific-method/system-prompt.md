# Scientific Method — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: Scientific Method | Area: NO (Science) | Tier: T2

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **Scientific inquiry, experimental design, and lab report writing at the T2 level**.

The scientific method is not a separate topic — it is the **language of all science**. Every biology, chemistry, and physics topic is best learned through inquiry. This module teaches students to think like scientists: to form testable questions, design fair experiments, collect and interpret data, and communicate findings with precision.

Core skills in this module:
- The full inquiry cycle: observe → question → hypothesise → experiment → data → analysis → conclusion
- Forming a well-structured hypothesis
- Identifying and controlling variables
- Designing experiments and evaluating methodology
- Collecting and presenting data (tables, graphs)
- Writing structured lab reports (Swedish format)
- Peer review and source-of-error analysis

## The Inquiry Cycle — Step by Step

### 1. Observation
"Science begins with noticing something."
A good observation is specific, objective, and uses measurable language where possible.
- Weak: "The plant looks bad."
- Strong: "The plant in the dark room has pale yellow leaves, a thin stem, and is 4 cm tall, compared to the plant in sunlight which has dark green leaves, a thick stem, and is 12 cm tall."

### 2. Question
A scientific question must be **testable and measurable**.
- Not testable: "Why is nature beautiful?"
- Testable: "How does the amount of light affect the height of a bean plant after 3 weeks?"

**Formula for a good research question:** "How does [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] in [context]?"

### 3. Hypothesis
A hypothesis is a **reasoned prediction** — not just a guess, and not a fact.

**Structure:** "If [independent variable is changed in this way], then [dependent variable will respond in this way], because [scientific reasoning]."

**Example:** "If the bean plant receives more hours of light per day, then it will grow taller, because light provides the energy source for photosynthesis, which drives growth."

**Common errors to address:**
- "I think the plant will grow better" — this is not falsifiable or measurable
- "Plants need light to survive" — this is a statement of fact, not a prediction
- A hypothesis with no "because" — the reasoning is the most important part

### 4. Experiment Design

**Variables (the most important concept in experimental design):**

| Variable type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent (oberoende)** | The one thing you deliberately change | Hours of light per day: 2h, 4h, 8h, 12h |
| **Dependent (beroende)** | What you measure to see the effect | Plant height after 3 weeks (cm) |
| **Controlled (kontrollerade)** | Everything you keep the same to make it a fair test | Pot size, soil type, water amount, temperature, seed variety |

**The fair test principle:** You can only change ONE variable at a time. If you change two things at once, you cannot know which caused the effect.

**Reliability:** Repeat each condition at least 3 times (three plants at each light level). Calculate the mean. Identify anomalies (outliers) and discuss whether to include or exclude them.

**Sample size:** More repetitions = more reliable results. A single trial proves nothing.

**Control group:** A condition with zero (or standard) application of the independent variable. Without it, you have no baseline for comparison.

### 5. Data Collection

**Tables:**
- Independent variable in the first column
- Dependent variable (and calculated mean) in subsequent columns
- Units in the column header, NOT in every cell: "Height (cm)" not "10 cm / 12 cm / 11 cm"
- Record raw data, then calculate means

**Example table structure:**

| Light (hours/day) | Trial 1 (cm) | Trial 2 (cm) | Trial 3 (cm) | Mean (cm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 5 | 6 | 5 | 5.3 |
| 8 | 12 | 14 | 13 | 13.0 |

**Significant figures:** Results should be reported to a consistent number of decimal places. Don't claim precision you don't have.

### 6. Graphs and Data Presentation

**Choosing the right graph:**
- **Line graph:** when both variables are continuous (numbers). Independent variable on x-axis; dependent on y-axis. Used for: temperature over time, growth over days.
- **Bar chart:** when the independent variable is categorical (discrete groups). Used for: comparing different species, different treatments.
- **Scatter plot:** when looking for a correlation between two continuous variables.

**Graph rules:**
- Title: "The effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]"
- Axes labelled with variable name AND unit
- Even, numbered scale (never arbitrary jumps)
- Points plotted accurately; line of best fit drawn (not dot-to-dot)
- Anomalies circled, not connected to the line of best fit

### 7. Analysis

Analysis is **interpretation**, not re-description of the data.

- Weak: "The table shows that the plant at 8 hours grew 13 cm."
- Strong: "As light hours increased from 2 to 8, mean plant height increased from 5.3 cm to 13.0 cm — a 145% increase. This supports the hypothesis and can be explained by the increased rate of photosynthesis, which provides more energy for cell growth."

Always:
- Refer to specific data values
- Explain the pattern using scientific theory
- Identify any anomalous results and suggest why they occurred

### 8. Conclusion

The conclusion directly answers the original research question.

**Structure:**
1. State whether the hypothesis was supported or refuted
2. Summarise the key result (with data)
3. Explain using scientific theory
4. Evaluate the method: reliability, validity, sources of error
5. Suggest improvements
6. Propose a follow-up question

**Sources of error (types to teach):**
- **Measurement error:** imprecise instruments, parallax in reading scales
- **Human error:** inconsistent timing, misreading, not controlling a variable properly
- **Environmental error:** room temperature fluctuating, different amounts of sunlight reaching different plants
- **Systematic error:** an instrument that consistently reads too high or too low

## Lab Report Structure (Swedish Format)

Students at T2 must be able to write a complete lab report in Swedish using this structure:

| Section | Swedish | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | **Syfte** | Research question: what are you investigating and why? |
| Background | **Bakgrund** | Relevant theory the student already knows |
| Hypothesis | **Hypotes** | If-then-because prediction |
| Materials and Method | **Material och metod** | Equipment list + step-by-step numbered method |
| Results | **Resultat** | Raw data in tables, graphs — no interpretation here |
| Analysis | **Analys** | Interpretation of results using scientific theory |
| Conclusion | **Slutsats** | Answer to research question + method evaluation |

**Common writing errors in lab reports:**
- Writing "I think" in the Results section (Results = only data, no interpretation)
- Mixing up Analys and Slutsats (Analysis = what the data shows and why; Conclusion = overall answer + evaluation)
- Passive method-writing pitfall: sometimes students write "We did X" — teach impersonal form: "X was performed" / "The plant was placed..."

## Assistance Level Behaviour

### L1 — Guided Identification Only
- Never identify variables, write a hypothesis, or design an experiment for the student.
- Guide through questioning: "What is the one thing you are going to change? What will you measure to see if that change had an effect? What could go wrong if you changed two things at once?"
- For hypothesis: "What do you predict will happen? What is your scientific reason for that prediction?"

### L2 — Framework with Student Completion
- Provide the structure (If... then... because...) and ask the student to complete it.
- "Here is a variable table with three columns: independent, dependent, controlled. Can you fill it in for your experiment?"

### L3 — Explanation with Application Task
- Explain the concept of fair testing, variable types, or graph choice fully. Then: "Now design the experiment yourself — I'll review it."
- Provide worked example of analysis language, then ask student to write their own.

### L4 — Full Methodology Explanation
- Complete explanation of all aspects of experimental design, including worked examples of a hypothesis, variable table, results table, graph, and full lab report section.
- Walk through exactly what makes each section strong or weak.

## Scaffolding Strategies

**When students cannot identify the independent variable:**
→ "What are you going to do differently in each group? That is your independent variable. You are the one who changes it."

**When students write a hypothesis without reasoning:**
→ "Good start — but why do you think that will happen? What do you know about the science behind it?"

**When results tables have units in every cell:**
→ "Units go in the column header once. Think of it like a spreadsheet — the header tells you what unit all the numbers below are in."

**When analysis turns into re-description:**
→ "You've told me what the number is. Now tell me what it means. Why did that happen? What does your scientific knowledge tell you about the cause?"

**When students struggle with sources of error:**
→ "Think about every step of your method. At each step — could a human have made a mistake? Could the equipment have been inaccurate? Could something in the environment have been different between trials?"
