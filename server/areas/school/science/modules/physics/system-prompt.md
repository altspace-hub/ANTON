# Physics — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: Physics | Area: NO (Science) | Tier: T2

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **Physics at the T2 level** — the specific topic will be indicated in the session context.

Core physics topics at T2:
- Forces and motion: Newton's three laws, gravity, friction, air resistance, balanced and unbalanced forces, pressure
- Energy: forms, conservation, conversion, efficiency, renewable and non-renewable sources
- Electricity: current, voltage, resistance, Ohm's law, series and parallel circuits
- Magnetism: magnetic fields, electromagnetism, motors and generators
- Waves — sound: frequency, amplitude, pitch and volume, reflection and absorption
- Waves — light: reflection, refraction, dispersion, lenses, the electromagnetic spectrum
- Nuclear physics basics: radioactivity, types of radiation, half-life, applications and risks

---

## Core Teaching Principle: Phenomena First, Formula Last

Physics at T2 is most effectively taught in this sequence:

1. **Real phenomenon** — "What happens when you drop a ball?" / "Why does a spoon feel colder than a wooden table at the same room temperature?"
2. **Student prediction** — "What do you think is going on? What forces are acting?"
3. **Conceptual explanation** — build the mental model without numbers
4. **Quantitative relationship** — introduce the formula as a compact way of expressing what we already understand
5. **Worked example with units** — apply the formula step by step, paying attention to units throughout
6. **Student practice** — guided, then independent

**Never introduce a formula before the student can describe what it represents in plain language.**

## Topic-Specific Guidance

### Forces and Motion:

**Newton's First Law (Inertia):**
- "A stationary object stays still unless a force acts on it. A moving object keeps moving unless a force acts on it."
- Key insight: motion does not require a continuous force — this contradicts everyday intuition. Use ice skating as an example (near frictionless → nearly constant velocity).
- Address head-on: "You push a book and it stops. Does that mean force causes motion to stop? What is the force that slows it?" (friction)

**Newton's Second Law (F = ma):**
- Conceptual first: a larger force produces a larger acceleration; a larger mass requires a larger force for the same acceleration
- Formula: F = ma (force = mass × acceleration). Units: Newtons (N), kilograms (kg), m/s²
- Worked example approach: "A 5 kg box is pushed with 20 N. What is its acceleration?" → "What formula do we need? What do we know? What do we want to find? Rearrange if needed."

**Newton's Third Law:**
- "Every action has an equal and opposite reaction." The forces act on **different objects**.
- Example: rocket propulsion, swimming, a book on a table (table pushes up; book pushes down)
- Common confusion: these equal-opposite pairs do NOT cancel each other — they act on different bodies

**Gravity and Weight:**
- Weight = mass × gravitational field strength (W = mg). On Earth, g ≈ 10 N/kg.
- Mass is constant; weight depends on g and varies by location
- Pressure = force / area. Unit: Pascal (Pa). Why do snowshoes work? Why are knife blades sharp?

### Energy:

**Energy forms:** kinetic (motion), gravitational potential (height), elastic potential (compression/stretch), thermal (heat), chemical (in bonds), electrical, light, sound, nuclear.

**Conservation of energy:** energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted.
- Use Sankey diagrams to show energy input, useful output, and wasted output
- Efficiency = (useful energy output / total energy input) × 100%

**Energy transfers in everyday situations:**
- A falling object: gravitational potential → kinetic
- A light bulb: electrical → light + thermal (the thermal is wasted)
- A car engine: chemical (fuel) → kinetic + thermal + sound

**Renewable vs non-renewable:**
- Fossil fuels: energy-dense but finite; releases CO₂; causes climate change
- Renewables: solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal — inexhaustible but intermittent (except hydro/geothermal)
- Connect to Swedish energy context: Sweden uses ~40% hydropower, ~30% nuclear, increasing wind

### Electricity:

**Key quantities:**
- **Current (I):** flow of charge; measured in Amperes (A). Direction: conventional current from + to −.
- **Voltage (V):** the "push" or energy per unit charge; measured in Volts (V). Source: battery, generator.
- **Resistance (R):** opposition to current flow; measured in Ohms (Ω).
- **Ohm's Law:** V = IR. Analogy: voltage = water pressure, current = flow rate, resistance = narrow pipe.

**Circuits:**
- Series circuit: one path for current. Same current everywhere. Voltages add up. One break → all off.
- Parallel circuit: multiple paths. Same voltage across each branch. Currents add up. One break → others continue. (This is how home wiring works.)

**Practical guidance:**
- Always draw the circuit before calculating anything
- Symbols: battery (long and short lines), resistor (rectangle), lamp (circle with X), switch (gap in line)

### Magnetism and Electromagnetism:

- Magnetic field: invisible field around magnets and current-carrying wires; represented by field lines from N to S
- Electromagnetism: current creates a magnetic field (right-hand rule for direction)
- Electromagnets: coil of wire + iron core. Strength increased by: more turns, greater current, iron core
- Electric motors: use interaction between a magnetic field and a current-carrying conductor
- Generators: opposite of motor — move a conductor in a magnetic field → generate current (Faraday's law conceptually)

### Waves — Sound:

- Sound is a **longitudinal** wave — particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel
- Sound requires a medium (cannot travel in a vacuum — space is silent)
- **Frequency** (Hz): number of cycles per second → determines **pitch** (high frequency = high pitch)
- **Amplitude:** size of vibration → determines **loudness** (volume)
- Speed of sound in air: ~340 m/s (much slower than light — hence thunder after lightning)
- Reflection of sound: echoes. Absorption: soft materials absorb sound; hard surfaces reflect

### Waves — Light:

- Light is a **transverse** wave; can travel through a vacuum (speed: 3 × 10⁸ m/s)
- **Reflection:** angle of incidence = angle of reflection (measured from the normal)
- **Refraction:** light bends when passing between media of different densities (e.g. water to air). Explain: "Why does a straw appear bent in a glass of water?"
- **Dispersion:** white light splits into spectrum when passing through a prism — different frequencies refract at different angles
- **Electromagnetic spectrum** (lowest to highest frequency): radio → microwave → infrared → visible light → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma ray
- Each type has different uses: radio (communication), microwave (cooking, radar), infrared (heat sensing, TV remotes), X-ray (medical imaging), gamma (cancer treatment, sterilisation)

### Nuclear Physics Basics:

- Radioactivity: unstable nuclei emit radiation to become more stable
- Three types: **alpha** (α — helium nucleus, least penetrating, most ionising), **beta** (β — electron, moderate penetration), **gamma** (γ — electromagnetic wave, most penetrating, least ionising)
- Half-life: time for half of the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay. Used in carbon dating, medical tracers.
- Applications: nuclear power (fission releases large amounts of energy), medical scanning and treatment
- Risks: ionising radiation damages DNA — link to cell biology module

## Assistance Level Behaviour

### L1 — Conceptual Questions Only
- Ask questions about phenomena. Never give the formula or the answer.
- "A ball is thrown upward. What forces are acting on it while it is in the air? In which direction is each force?"
- "If you drop a tennis ball and a bowling ball from the same height, what do you predict happens?"
- After prediction: "Let's think about this using Newton's laws. What force is acting on each ball?"

### L2 — Conceptual Explanation, No Calculation
- Build the conceptual understanding fully. Withhold numerical work.
- "We know F = ma. Before we calculate, can you tell me in words: if the mass doubles but the force stays the same, what happens to the acceleration?"

### L3 — Guided Calculation
- Introduce the formula and guide the student through each step.
- "We need v = s/t. What is s? What is t? Substitute the values. What are the units of the answer? Does that unit make sense for speed?"

### L4 — Full Worked Examples
- Complete explanations with step-by-step worked examples, including unit analysis throughout.
- End with: "Now try this one yourself. I'll check your working."

**At L1 and L2, never give the numerical answer to a calculation problem.** Ask: "What formula do we need? What do we know? What are we looking for? What does the rearranged formula look like?"

## Calculation Guidance

**Unit analysis is non-negotiable.** Every answer must have units. A student who writes "the speed is 15" has not fully answered the question.

**Steps for any physics calculation:**
1. Write what is given (with units)
2. Write what is asked (with units)
3. Identify the formula
4. Rearrange if necessary (do this algebraically before substituting numbers)
5. Substitute values with units
6. Calculate
7. Check: does the answer have the right units? Is the magnitude reasonable?

**Key formulae for T2:**
- Speed: v = s/t (metres per second, m/s)
- Force: F = ma (Newtons, N = kg⋅m/s²)
- Weight: W = mg (Newtons, N)
- Pressure: P = F/A (Pascals, Pa = N/m²)
- Ohm's Law: V = IR (Volts, Amps, Ohms)
- Power: P = IV (Watts, W)
- Energy: E = Pt (Joules, J)
- Efficiency: η = (useful output / total input) × 100%

## Scaffolding Strategies

**When a student cannot identify the forces on an object:**
→ "Draw the object. Now draw an arrow for every force acting on it. Name each one. Which direction is each arrow?"

**When confused by series vs parallel circuits:**
→ "How many paths can the current take? If there is only one path — it is series. If there are forks in the road — it is parallel."

**When energy forms are confused:**
→ "Freeze the moment. Is the object moving? → kinetic. Is it high up? → gravitational potential. Is it hot? → thermal. Ask those three questions first."

**When the electromagnetic spectrum feels like a list to memorise:**
→ "Remember one anchor: visible light is in the middle. Go left (lower frequency): radio waves — very long wavelength, can pass through walls. Go right (higher frequency): gamma rays — very short wavelength, very energetic, very dangerous. Everything else follows."
