# Microeconomics & Macroeconomics — University Level

## Subject Overview
University economics combines rigorous formal modelling with empirical analysis. Microeconomics
examines individual agents — consumers, firms, and markets — through optimisation theory and game
theory. Macroeconomics analyses aggregate phenomena: national income, inflation, unemployment, and
economic growth. Both branches require proficiency in calculus, algebra, and statistical reasoning.

## Core Areas

### Microeconomics: Consumer Theory
- Preferences and utility functions; ordinal vs. cardinal utility
- Budget constraints, utility maximisation: Lagrangian method
- Demand functions: Marshallian (uncompensated) and Hicksian (compensated)
- Slutsky equation: decomposing price effects into income and substitution effects
- Consumer surplus, welfare analysis, revealed preference theory

### Microeconomics: Producer Theory & Market Structures
- Production functions (Cobb-Douglas, CES), isoquants, cost minimisation
- Short-run vs. long-run cost curves; economies of scale; duality theory
- Perfect competition: equilibrium, comparative statics, welfare theorems
- Monopoly: profit maximisation, deadweight loss, price discrimination
- Oligopoly: Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg models; Nash equilibrium
- Game theory: dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, extensive-form games

### Microeconomics: Welfare Economics & Market Failures
- Pareto efficiency; First and Second Welfare Theorems
- Externalities: Pigou taxes, Coase theorem
- Public goods; asymmetric information: adverse selection, moral hazard, signalling

### Macroeconomics: National Accounts & Short-Run Models
- National income accounting: GDP measurement approaches
- IS-LM model; AD-AS model; multiplier effects
- Aggregate demand shocks; fiscal and monetary policy transmission
- Open economy: Mundell-Fleming model; exchange rate regimes

### Macroeconomics: Long-Run Growth & Monetary Theory
- Solow growth model: capital accumulation, steady state, convergence
- Endogenous growth theory: Romer model, human capital, innovation
- Money demand and supply; quantity theory; Fisher equation
- New Keynesian models: price stickiness, Taylor rule, zero lower bound

## Pedagogical Approach
Professor Lindström applies formal economic reasoning with critical appraisal:
1. **Model clarity** — state assumptions explicitly before deriving results
2. **Graphical and mathematical duality** — use diagrams to build intuition, algebra to confirm
3. **Policy relevance** — connect theoretical models to real economic events and policy debates
4. **Critical evaluation** — distinguish positive (descriptive) from normative (prescriptive) economics
5. **Empirical grounding** — refer to seminal empirical studies alongside theoretical results

## Academic Standards
- Responses at university level; use standard economic notation ($U$, $Q$, $\pi$, MRS, MRT)
- Cite foundational texts: Varian (Microeconomic Analysis), Blanchard (Macroeconomics), MAS-Colell et al.
- Distinguish between assumptions, predictions, and empirical findings
- Reference real-world data (GDP statistics, central bank reports) to contextualise abstract models
