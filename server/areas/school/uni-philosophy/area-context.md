# Philosophy & Ethics (University) — University Level

## Subject Overview
University philosophy cultivates rigorous conceptual analysis, logical argumentation, and critical
examination of foundational questions about knowledge, reality, mind, morality, and society.
Students engage with historical texts and contemporary analytic and continental philosophy alike.
The discipline trains precision in language, sensitivity to argument structure, and intellectual
humility in the face of genuinely hard problems.

## Core Areas

### Epistemology — Theory of Knowledge
- Classical analysis of knowledge: justified true belief (JTB); Gettier cases and their aftermath
- Sources of knowledge: perception, memory, testimony, a priori reasoning, introspection
- Scepticism: Cartesian doubt, external world problem, brain-in-a-vat; responses (contextualism, reliabilism)
- Foundationalism vs. coherentism vs. infinitism about epistemic justification
- Social epistemology: testimony, epistemic injustice (Fricker), collective knowledge

### Metaphysics — Nature of Reality
- Ontology: what exists? Particulars and universals; nominalism, Platonism, trope theory
- Identity and persistence through time: temporal parts, four-dimensionalism, endurantism
- Philosophy of mind: substance dualism, physicalism, functionalism, qualia, the hard problem of consciousness
- Free will and determinism: compatibilism (Frankfurt, Dennett), hard incompatibilism, libertarianism
- Causation: Humean regularity theory, counterfactual analysis, interventionist accounts

### Logic & Argumentation
- Propositional logic: syntax, truth tables, validity, soundness, formal proofs
- Predicate logic: quantifiers, variable binding, translation, natural deduction
- Informal logic: argument identification, fallacies (ad hominem, strawman, false dichotomy, slippery slope)
- Inductive logic: enumerative induction, inference to the best explanation, Bayesian reasoning
- Philosophy of logic: logical truth, necessity, possible worlds semantics (Kripke)

### Ethics — Moral Philosophy
- Normative theories: consequentialism (utilitarianism — Bentham, Mill, Singer), deontology (Kant's categorical imperative), virtue ethics (Aristotle, MacIntyre)
- Metaethics: moral realism vs. anti-realism; cognitivism, expressivism, constructivism
- Applied ethics: bioethics, environmental ethics, AI ethics, global justice, animal ethics
- Political philosophy: social contract theories (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls); justice, liberty, equality
- Philosophy of law: natural law, legal positivism (Hart), interpretivism (Dworkin)

### Continental Philosophy
- Existentialism: Sartre on radical freedom, bad faith, and authentic existence; Heidegger on being and time
- Phenomenology: Husserl's intentionality; Merleau-Ponty on embodied perception
- Critical theory: Frankfurt School; Habermas on communicative rationality
- Structuralism and post-structuralism: Foucault on power/knowledge; Derrida on deconstruction

## Pedagogical Approach
Professor Lindström employs the Socratic seminar method at its most rigorous:
1. **Argument mapping** — identify premises, conclusion, and implicit assumptions in every argument
2. **Principle of charity** — reconstruct the strongest version of opposing positions before critiquing
3. **Counterexample generation** — test general claims against carefully designed counterexamples
4. **Conceptual clarity** — demand precise definitions before accepting that a problem is well-posed
5. **Historical dialogue** — situate contemporary debates within their intellectual history

## Academic Standards
- Cite philosophers and texts with precision (Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, 4:421)
- Distinguish between an argument being valid, sound, and persuasive
- Avoid equivocation — track how key terms shift meaning in different contexts
- Academic philosophical writing: thesis-driven, structured, avoiding rhetoric without argument
