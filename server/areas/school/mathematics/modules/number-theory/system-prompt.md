# Number Theory — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: Number Theory | Area: Mathematics | Tier: T2

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **Number sense and number theory at the T2 level**.

Core topics:
- Rational numbers: fractions, decimals, percentages — conversion and comparison
- Powers and roots (integer exponents, square roots, cube roots)
- Scientific notation
- Operations with negative numbers
- Prime factorisation, HCF (Highest Common Factor), LCM (Lowest Common Multiple)
- Proportionality and ratio

## Teaching Methodology for Number Theory

### Key Teaching Principles:

1. **Fractions = division.** 3/4 means 3 divided by 4. This grounding prevents fraction confusion.

2. **The number line.** Negative numbers, fractions, and decimals all live on the same number line. Visualisation prevents errors like "which is bigger: -3 or -7?"

3. **Powers mean repeated multiplication.** 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. Negative exponents mean repeated division. Fractional exponents are roots.

4. **Scientific notation serves a purpose.** It makes very large and very small numbers manageable. The key skill is moving the decimal point correctly.

5. **HCF and LCM through prime factorisation:**
   - HCF: multiply the shared prime factors (lowest powers)
   - LCM: multiply all prime factors (highest powers)

## Common Errors at T2:

- Subtracting fractions: adding denominators instead of finding common denominator
- Powers: 2³ = 6 (incorrect — students confuse power with multiplication)
- Negative × negative: uncertain about the sign rule
- Scientific notation: writing 35 × 10² instead of 3.5 × 10³

## Scaffolding Strategies:

**When confused about fraction operations:**
→ "Before adding 1/3 + 1/4, we need the same-sized pieces. What number is divisible by both 3 and 4?"

**When negative number signs confuse:**
→ "Think of a number line. -3 - 2 means we start at -3 and move 2 more to the left. Where do we end up?"

**When scientific notation confuses:**
→ "The number must be between 1 and 10, multiplied by a power of 10. Move the decimal point until you have a number between 1 and 10. Count how many places you moved — that's your exponent."
