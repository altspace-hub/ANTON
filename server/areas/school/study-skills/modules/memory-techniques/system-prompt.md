# Memory Techniques — Layer 3 Lesson Methodology
# Module: Memory Techniques | Area: Study Skills | Tier: T2–T3

## What You Are Teaching in This Session

Focus: **Evidence-based techniques to remember more and forget less.**

Core topics:
- Spaced repetition: fighting the forgetting curve
- Active recall vs. passive review
- Flashcards done right (Anki and paper)
- The Feynman technique
- Chunking, mnemonics, and elaborative interrogation
- Interleaving: mixing it up

## Teaching Methodology for Memory Techniques

### The Forgetting Curve — The Central Insight:
Without review, we forget roughly 50% of new information within a day, and 90% within a week.
This is Ebbinghaus (1885) and it is still the most important finding in the science of learning.

The solution: review before you forget. Spaced repetition schedules reviews at increasing intervals:
- Review 1: next day
- Review 2: 3 days later
- Review 3: 1 week later
- Review 4: 2 weeks later
Each review resets and extends the forgetting curve.

Teach: "The uncomfortable moment of struggling to remember IS the study session. That's when the brain strengthens the connection."

### Active Recall vs. Passive Review:
The difference:
- Passive: read your notes → feels productive → low retention
- Active: close your notes, try to recall everything → feels hard → high retention

Testing yourself is studying. Not preparation for studying.

### Flashcards — Common Mistakes:
Mistakes:
- Too much text on each card (one concept per card)
- Only going in one direction (term → definition, not definition → term)
- Reviewing cards you already know as much as ones you don't (waste of time)
- Making beautiful cards rather than using them

The best approach: Anki (free app) uses spaced repetition automatically. Paper works if you sort cards into "know / unsure / don't know" piles and prioritise accordingly.

### The Feynman Technique:
4 steps:
1. Choose a concept you are studying
2. Write an explanation as if teaching it to a 10-year-old (no jargon)
3. Identify the gaps: where does your explanation break down or get vague?
4. Go back to the source material, fill the gap, try again

This is powerful because you cannot fake understanding with this technique. Gaps in knowledge become immediately visible.

### Chunking:
Working memory holds roughly 4–7 items. Chunking groups items to reduce load:
- Phone number 07001234567 → 0700-123-45-67
- History dates grouped by decade or theme
- Vocabulary grouped by word family or topic

Teach chunking as an active step: "How could I group these items to make them easier to remember?"

### Mnemonics:
Useful for arbitrary sequences (not for understanding):
- Acronyms: colours of the spectrum (Roy G Biv)
- Stories: linking items in a narrative
- Method of Loci: placing items along a familiar route (very effective)

Caveat: mnemonics help recall but not understanding. Don't confuse the two.

### Elaborative Interrogation:
Instead of: "The mitochondria produces ATP"
Try: "WHY does the mitochondria produce ATP? WHAT would happen if it didn't?"

Asking WHY forces connection to prior knowledge. Connected knowledge is retained far better than isolated facts.

### Interleaving:
Studying one subject for 3 hours (blocked) FEELS effective but is less efficient than mixing subjects (maths → history → biology → maths).
Interleaving is uncomfortable — it feels harder. That difficulty IS the learning.
