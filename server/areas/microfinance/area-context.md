# Microfinance & Financial Inclusion — Area Context

## Domain Landscape

Microfinance provides small-scale financial services — primarily credit, but increasingly savings, insurance, and payments — to low-income individuals and micro-enterprises excluded from conventional banking. The sector serves approximately 140 million borrowers globally through over 10,000 institutions ranging from large regulated commercial microbanks to small community-based cooperatives and NGO-MFIs.

The sector's intellectual foundation rests on the 1983 work of Muhammad Yunus at Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, demonstrating that poor borrowers are creditworthy when appropriate products and structures are applied. Yunus received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006, bringing global attention to microfinance as a development tool.

## Sector Scale and Geographies

**South Asia** dominates by borrower count, with India alone accounting for approximately 60 million active borrowers through NBFC-MFIs regulated by the Reserve Bank of India. Bangladesh hosts brac (the world's largest NGO), Grameen Bank, and ASA. Pakistan's microfinance sector is supervised by the Securities and Exchange Commission.

**Sub-Saharan Africa** is characterised by diversity of institutional form: NGO-MFIs (Kenya Women Microfinance Bank, BRAC Uganda), cooperatives (SACCOs in East Africa serving millions), and licensed microbanks (Equity Bank Kenya, which originated as a building society serving low-income borrowers). The African Development Bank and IFC are key funders of the sector.

**Latin America** has the most commercially-oriented microfinance sector, with regulated microbanks in Bolivia (BancoSol, the world's first commercialised MFI), Peru (Mibanco), Mexico (Compartamos), and Colombia. The sector has faced client protection crises in several markets linked to over-indebtedness and aggressive collection practices.

## Institutional Models

Four primary institutional models exist, each with distinct regulatory treatment:

1. **NGO-MFI** — Non-profit, cannot take deposits, funded by grants and loans, exempt from or lightly regulated under banking law. Common in early-stage markets (Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia).
2. **Licensed MFI / NBFC-MFI** — Regulated non-bank financial company, credit-only or limited deposits. Primary form in India (RBI NBFC-MFI regulations), Kenya (CBK Microfinance Act 2006), and Nigeria (CBN Microfinance Policy 2005/2011).
3. **Cooperative / SACCO** — Member-owned, regulated under cooperative law rather than banking law. Dominant in East Africa (SACCOs regulate through SACCO Societies Regulatory Authority in Kenya).
4. **Commercial Microbank** — Full banking licence with focus on micro/SME market. Highest regulatory burden but full deposit-taking capability.

## Social Mission vs. Commercial Sustainability

The fundamental tension in microfinance is between the social mission of serving the poorest clients and the operational sustainability required to scale. International funders including the World Bank, IFC Microfinance, and bilateral donors have increasingly demanded both social performance and financial returns.

This tension produced the sector's major crisis: in 2010, the Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis in India saw over-indebted borrowers, aggressive collection practices, and suicides, triggering a regulatory crackdown that nearly collapsed the sector. Similar crises occurred in Morocco (2008), Bosnia (2009), and Nicaragua (2010).

## International Standards and Frameworks

- **Smart Campaign / Client Protection Principles** — Seven principles for responsible treatment of micro-clients: appropriate product design, prevention of over-indebtedness, transparency, responsible pricing, fair treatment, privacy, and complaints resolution. Now absorbed into the SPTF Universal Standards.
- **Social Performance Task Force (SPTF)** — Publishes Universal Standards for Social Performance Management (USSPM), the sector's primary social performance framework.
- **CERISE SPI4** — Social Performance Indicators tool, practical audit methodology aligned to USSPM.
- **Principles for Investors in Inclusive Finance (PIIF)** — Investor-side responsible finance framework.
- **AFI (Alliance for Financial Inclusion)** — Network of 90+ central banks and regulators from developing countries; publishes the Maya Declaration and Financial Inclusion Strategy (FSI) measurement framework.
- **UNSGSA** — UN Secretary-General's Special Advocate for Inclusive Finance for Development; publishes annual reports on financial inclusion progress.
- **G20 Global Partnership for Financial Inclusion (GPFI)** — High-level political framework linking G20 to financial inclusion commitments.

## Islamic Microfinance

A significant and growing sub-sector serves Muslim-majority communities for whom conventional interest-based products are religiously prohibited. Islamic microfinance uses Sharia-compliant product structures:
- **Qard Hasan** — benevolent loan with no interest; service fees permitted
- **Murabaha** — cost-plus deferred sale used for asset/inventory finance
- **Ijara** — leasing structure for productive assets
- **Musharakah** — partnership arrangement sharing profits and losses

Pakistan, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Sudan, and Nigeria are key markets. AAOIFI (Accounting and Auditing Organisation for Islamic Financial Institutions) and IFSB (Islamic Financial Services Board) publish the primary Sharia governance standards.

## Key Terminology

- **PAR (Portfolio At Risk)** — percentage of portfolio with payments more than X days overdue; primary credit quality indicator
- **OSS (Operational Self-Sufficiency)** — operational revenue divided by operating expenses; sustainability threshold
- **Write-off Ratio** — loans written off as percentage of average gross loan portfolio
- **Gross Loan Portfolio (GLP)** — total outstanding loan principal
- **COGS** — Cost of Goods Sold (used in Murabaha pricing)
- **SHG** — Self-Help Group; group savings and lending model prevalent in India
- **VSLA** — Village Savings and Loan Association; community-managed model common in Sub-Saharan Africa
- **Over-indebtedness** — borrower has more debt than can be sustainably serviced; primary client protection risk
