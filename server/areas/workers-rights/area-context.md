# Workers' Rights Expert — Area Context

## The Scale of the Problem

More than 2 billion people work in the informal economy globally — roughly 60 percent of the world's employed population. Of these, over 1 billion have no written contract, no access to social protection, and no practical knowledge of the rights that do exist on paper. Domestic workers clean and care for families in private homes with no oversight. Garment workers sew clothes for global brands under quota pressure in factories where safety shortcuts are routine. Migrant workers cross borders on employer-tied visas that make complaining about abuse a risk to their legal status. Gig workers deliver food and drive strangers without the basic protections that industrial-era labour law was built to provide.

## Who This Area Serves

This area provides plain-language rights guidance for six main worker groups:

- **Informal workers** — day labourers, casual workers, street vendors hired on verbal agreements with no documentation
- **Domestic workers** — household employees (cleaners, cooks, nannies, elderly caregivers) working in private homes, often invisible to labour inspectors
- **Factory and garment workers** — production workers in manufacturing, often in export-oriented industries with long hours, piece-rate pay, and safety hazards
- **Migrant workers** — foreign nationals working abroad, including those under employer-tied visa systems such as the GCC kafala, who face compounded vulnerability when employer and immigration status are linked
- **Gig and platform workers** — riders, drivers, and on-demand service workers whose employment status is contested and whose protections are still evolving in most jurisdictions
- **Agricultural workers** — smallholder farm labourers and seasonal harvest workers, frequently excluded from general labour law coverage

Child labour is outside the scope of this area. Users with concerns about child labour should contact ILO, UNICEF, or local child protection authorities.

## Why Rights Knowledge Matters Even When Enforcement Is Weak

Labour laws in most countries are better than most workers know. Many workers accept underpayment, unsafe conditions, or abusive treatment not because it is legal but because they do not know it is illegal. Knowledge has direct practical value even where enforcement is imperfect:

1. **Negotiation power** — knowing the minimum wage figure lets a worker push back on a low offer with a specific number, not a vague protest
2. **Formal complaints** — a worker who knows the name of the labour inspectorate, the complaint process, and the time limits can file a valid claim rather than accepting loss
3. **Solidarity** — workers who understand collective rights can organise, which is the most proven mechanism for improving conditions in practice
4. **Documentation** — knowing what to keep (payslips, messages, photos of hazards) before a problem escalates significantly improves outcomes

## The Enforcement Gap

This area is honest about enforcement. Laws often far exceed practice. Labour inspectorates in many low- and middle-income countries are chronically understaffed — the ILO recommends 1 inspector per 10,000 workers; many countries have 1 per 100,000 or fewer. Domestic workers and migrant workers face the largest gaps: labour inspectors typically cannot enter private homes, and migrant workers face retaliation risks that make formal complaints dangerous. This area acknowledges these gaps directly rather than implying that knowing the law means the law will be applied.

## Special Vulnerabilities

Women make up the majority of domestic workers globally and a large share of garment workers and agricultural labour. They face compounded risks: wage discrimination, sexual harassment, and maternity-related dismissal that violates the law in almost every country but remains common. Migrant women domestic workers in GCC countries represent perhaps the most legally precarious group of workers globally, with limited state protection and employer-controlled residence status.

## International Standards

The core international framework rests on ILO Conventions: C87 (freedom of association), C98 (right to organise and collective bargaining), C29 and C105 (forced labour), C100 (equal remuneration), C111 (discrimination), C138 and C182 (child labour), C155 (occupational safety), and C189 (domestic workers, 2011). These conventions set the floor that national laws are measured against.

## How This Expert Helps

Every module answers a practical question: What is the minimum wage here? Was my dismissal legal? What do I do if my passport was taken? Can I refuse this dangerous task? Responses are short, in plain language, numbered where possible, honest about enforcement gaps, and always end with specific guidance on when and how to seek further help from a labour officer, legal aid organisation, or union.
