# Land & Property Rights Expert — Area Context

## Land as the Foundation of Livelihoods

For the majority of rural families across Africa and South Asia, land is not just property — it is the primary productive asset, the basis of food security, and the anchor of family identity across generations. A family that loses its land loses almost everything. Yet globally, over 70 percent of land in Sub-Saharan Africa has no formal title documentation. Hundreds of millions of people live on and farm land they cannot prove is theirs in any court.

## The Scale of the Problem

Land insecurity takes many forms. In rapidly growing cities, families who have lived on land for decades face eviction by developers or government projects without fair compensation. In rural areas, widows are expelled from family land by in-laws the day after a husband dies — a practice that is illegal under the law of most countries but deeply entrenched in customary practice. In high-growth corridors, speculators forge title deeds and sell land to multiple buyers simultaneously. Smallholder farmers discover that their plot boundaries have quietly shifted after a neighbour hires a corrupt surveyor. Governments expropriate land for infrastructure projects with compensation that is far below market value.

## Three Legal Systems in Tension

In most of Africa and South Asia, land rights are governed by three overlapping and often contradictory legal systems:

- **Statutory law** — national legislation passed by parliament. Generally provides the strongest protections, especially for women. But statutory law often exists only on paper in rural areas where formal courts are distant and expensive.
- **Customary law** — traditional rules governing land within clans, tribes, or communities. Often patrilineal, meaning land passes through men. Widely practised even where it contradicts statutory law.
- **Religious law** — particularly Islamic land law, which includes detailed inheritance rules (Quranic shares) and the concept of waqf (religious endowment). Relevant in Muslim-majority regions of Nigeria, northern Tanzania, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and parts of India.

Courts in most countries give statutory law priority. But custom shapes daily reality, and most people never reach a court.

## Women and Land

Women's land rights are the largest single gap between law and practice in this area. In most countries, women have the same statutory right to own, inherit, and use land as men. In practice, customary systems deny these rights routinely. A woman who leaves a marriage — whether through divorce or widowhood — is often left with nothing, regardless of what the law says. The practical protective steps — joint registration, early documentation, witness statements — are available and make a real difference, but most women are never told about them.
