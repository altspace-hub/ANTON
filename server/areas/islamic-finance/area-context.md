# Islamic Finance & Banking — Area Context

## What Is Islamic Finance?

Islamic finance refers to financial services and products structured in accordance with Sharia (Islamic law). It is not merely a religious niche — it is a fully functioning alternative financial system with its own theoretical foundations, regulatory infrastructure, accounting standards, and supervisory architecture. The system prohibits charging or paying interest (riba), prohibits transactions with excessive uncertainty (gharar), and prohibits involvement in gambling or speculative transactions (maysir). In their place, Islamic finance uses profit-and-loss sharing, asset-backed structures, and tangible economic activity as the foundation for financial transactions.

## Industry Size and Geography

The global Islamic finance industry is estimated at approximately USD 6 trillion in assets, growing at 8–10% annually. It is no longer confined to Muslim-majority countries — Islamic finance assets are held in over 80 jurisdictions, with significant presence in Europe (UK, Luxembourg), the United States, and Sub-Saharan Africa.

The primary markets are:

- **Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC):** Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and Oman account for the largest share of global Islamic banking assets. Saudi Arabia's banking sector is now majority Islamic. The UAE hosts the Dubai Islamic Economy initiative and is a leading sukuk listing venue.
- **Malaysia:** The most institutionally developed Islamic finance ecosystem globally, with a dual banking system, a sophisticated regulatory framework under Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM), the world's largest sukuk market, and globally respected Sharia scholarship.
- **Pakistan:** Committed to full Islamisation of its banking system by 2027 under Supreme Court orders; the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) has an active conversion programme.
- **Indonesia:** The world's largest Muslim population is driving rapid growth; OJK (Otoritas Jasa Keuangan) oversees Islamic finance regulation and the country is developing its sovereign sukuk programme.
- **Other growth markets:** Bangladesh, Turkey, Kazakhstan, Egypt, Nigeria, and East Africa are all experiencing rapid Islamic finance growth.

## Core Sharia Principles

Three prohibitions underpin Islamic finance:

1. **Riba (Interest):** Any predetermined, fixed return on a loan or debt is prohibited. Money must not generate money by itself — returns must arise from productive economic activity, shared risk, or asset ownership.
2. **Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty):** Contracts must be clear, with defined subject matter, price, and delivery terms. Options (in their conventional form), short-selling, and certain derivatives are restricted.
3. **Maysir (Speculation/Gambling):** Transactions must have a productive economic purpose. Pure speculative instruments without underlying economic substance are prohibited.

## Key Islamic Finance Products

- **Murabaha:** Cost-plus financing. The financier purchases an asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments. Widely used for trade finance and consumer lending.
- **Ijara:** Lease financing. The bank purchases and leases an asset to the customer, with or without an option to purchase at the end. The Islamic equivalent of leasing/hire-purchase.
- **Musharakah:** Partnership. Both bank and customer contribute capital and share profits and losses according to a pre-agreed ratio. Used for project finance and equity participation.
- **Mudarabah:** Silent partnership. One party provides capital; the other provides expertise and management. Profits are shared; losses fall on the capital provider only (absent misconduct). Used for investment accounts and fund structures.
- **Sukuk:** Islamic capital market instruments often described as "Islamic bonds," though they represent ownership in underlying assets or usufruct rights rather than pure debt. The global sukuk market exceeds USD 800 billion outstanding.
- **Tawarruq:** Commodity-based monetisation. A client purchases a commodity on deferred payment then sells it spot for cash. Used for liquidity management and personal financing, though subject to scholarly debate.

## Regulatory Standards Bodies

- **AAOIFI (Accounting and Auditing Organisation for Islamic Financial Institutions):** Bahrain-based standard-setter for Sharia standards (48+), accounting standards, auditing standards, and governance standards. Adopted as mandatory in Bahrain, Jordan, Qatar, Sudan, Syria, and others; used as reference globally.
- **IFSB (Islamic Financial Services Board):** Malaysia-based prudential standard-setter for Islamic banking, takaful, and capital markets. Issues standards on capital adequacy, liquidity, risk management, corporate governance (IFSB-10 on Sharia governance), and supervisory review.
- **National Sharia Boards:** Many jurisdictions operate a national Sharia supervisory body — BNM's Sharia Advisory Council (SAC) in Malaysia, the Higher Sharia Committee under SAMA in Saudi Arabia, the UAE's Higher Sharia Authority — whose rulings are legally binding within their jurisdictions.

## Why This Matters for Financial Institutions and Regulators

Islamic financial institutions (IFIs) face a dual compliance challenge: they must meet both conventional prudential and conduct requirements AND Sharia compliance requirements, which have their own audit trail, governance structures, and liability implications. A Sharia non-compliance finding can void contracts retroactively, expose the institution to legal risk, and result in significant reputational damage.

Conventional banks operating Islamic windows face additional ring-fencing, governance, and liquidity management challenges. Regulators in dual-system jurisdictions must develop the supervisory capacity to assess both dimensions simultaneously.

## Connection to FCP, ESG, and Accounting

**Financial Crime Prevention:** Islamic finance is not immune to AML/CFT risk. FATF has published guidance on ML/TF risks in Islamic finance, noting that the complexity of structures (such as multi-commodity Murabaha chains) can create opacity. Zakat collection and waqf administration also present specific AML considerations. Islamic financial institutions must integrate Sharia compliance with robust FCP frameworks.

**ESG and Sustainable Finance:** The convergence between Islamic finance and ESG is natural — both share a values-based, long-term, asset-backed philosophy. Green sukuk (Islamic bonds for environmental projects) has become the fastest-growing segment of the sustainable finance market. The ICMA Green Bond Principles, Climate Bonds Standards, and AAOIFI Sharia standards can be applied simultaneously.

**Accounting:** AAOIFI Financial Accounting Standards (FAS) differ significantly from IFRS in how they treat Murabaha, Ijara, and investment accounts. Institutions reporting under both standards must carefully manage differences in revenue recognition, asset classification, and off-balance-sheet treatment.
