The Missions pillar — supervised multi-step automation

A Mission is a job too big for a single prompt: research, outreach, monitoring — work that runs across many steps, sometimes over days. ANTON does the steps; you stay in control through checkpoints where a human must approve before it goes on. It is automation with a hand on the brake.

1What a Mission is

A Work module answers one question well. A Mission is different in kind: it is a programme of work broken into a sequence of tasks, where each task may produce something the next one needs, and some tasks reach outside ANTON — sending an email, querying a service, monitoring a feed.

Because a Mission acts in the world and runs unattended between your visits, it is built around checkpoints: defined points where the Mission pauses, shows you what it has done and what it proposes to do next, and waits for your approval. Nothing irreversible happens without a human saying yes. That proposal/confirmation discipline is the heart of the pillar.

⛓

Multi-step

A Mission is a sequence of tasks — research, outreach, monitoring — not a single prompt-and-answer.

⌸

Checkpointed

At each checkpoint the Mission proposes; a human confirms before it proceeds. You are always the gate.

🔐

Credentialled

Outside actions use credentials from an encrypted vault — never pasted into a prompt, never logged.

2How to think about a Mission

🧭The mental model

Think of a Mission as delegating to a capable junior, not pressing a "do everything" button. A good junior works through a plan, comes back at the milestones that matter — "here is what I found, here is what I'd do next, is that right?" — and never does the consequential thing without checking. The checkpoints are exactly those come-back moments. Missions automate the effort, not the judgement.

Two ideas keep Missions safe and useful:

  • Checkpoints are where you do your job. A Mission is only as good as the attention you give it at each checkpoint. Read the proposal, not just the summary, before you confirm — that is the whole point of the pause.
  • The vault is a boundary. A Mission can only reach a service if you have given it a credential in the vault. Granting a credential is granting reach — treat it as deliberately as you would handing over a key.
💡Start from a Mission template

The Missions catalogue ships pre-built templates — multi-task programmes that already have their checkpoints placed in the right spots. Unless your job is unusual, start from the closest template and adapt it; you inherit a sensible task breakdown and a checkpoint plan that has been thought through.

Four pieces make Missions work. You meet all four on screen.

The inbox is the pillar's centre of gravity. A Mission running quietly in the background surfaces a card in the inbox each time it reaches a checkpoint: here is the step, here is the evidence, here is the proposal — approve, adjust or stop. The same checkpoints can also reach you on the Companion app, where the approvals inbox lets you confirm from your phone.

How it works Mission services and routes +

The Missions UI lives in src/pages/missions/ — MissionsCatalogPage.tsx (templates), MissionCreatorPage.tsx, MissionDashboardPage.tsx, MissionInboxPage.tsx (the checkpoint queue), CredentialVaultPage.tsx and ServicePacksPage.tsx.

Server-side, server/services/missions/ holds the engine: mission-controller.ts and mission-state.ts drive the lifecycle, mission-decomposition.ts breaks a job into tasks, mission-executor.ts (with executors/) runs them, mission-checkpoint.ts handles the proposal/confirmation gates, mission-credential-vault.ts encrypts credentials, service-pack-manager.ts manages service packs and mission-budget.ts caps spend. The REST surface is the server/routes/mission-*.ts family — missions.ts, mission-credentials.ts, mission-delivery.ts, mission-delegation.ts and more.

4Running a Mission, step by step

  1. Pick a Mission from the catalogue

    Choose a template that matches your programme — or create one from scratch and let ANTON decompose it into tasks.

  2. Set the scope and goal

    Tell the Mission what success looks like and any boundaries — scope, audience, budget. This shapes every task.

  3. Provision credentials

    If the Mission needs to act outside ANTON, add the needed credentials to the vault and attach the relevant service packs.

  4. Start the Mission

    ANTON works through the tasks. It runs unattended between checkpoints — you don't need to watch it.

  5. Clear checkpoints from the inbox

    Each checkpoint lands as an inbox card: read the proposal and the evidence, then approve, adjust or stop.

  6. Collect the delivery

    When the final task completes, the Mission delivers its result — ready to review and export.

⚠Checkpoints are not a formality

It is tempting to approve checkpoints quickly to keep a Mission moving. Don't. A checkpoint exists precisely because the next step is consequential — it sends something, commits something, or builds on a judgement that could be wrong. Read the proposal. The Mission cannot un-send an email you waved through.

5Worked example — the AMLR readiness template

The clearest way to see Missions is the built-in template tmpl_amlr_readiness_v1 — a complete, end-to-end programme for an entity that falls under the AMLR.

📌Example — a 10-task AMLR programme with 4 checkpoints

You are getting an obliged entity ready for the AMLR. From the catalogue you start the AMLR readiness Mission. It is a 10-task programme: scope the obligations → build a Risk Atlas → run the business-wide risk assessment → analyse gaps → draft policies → prepare training → set up the audit, and the connecting steps between them. Four explicit checkpoints are placed at the moments that matter — after scoping, after the risk assessment, after the gap analysis, and before the audit package is finalised. ANTON works each task; when it reaches a checkpoint it pauses and a card appears in your inbox. At the gap-analysis checkpoint you read the 12 gaps it found, agree they're right, and approve it to draft remediation policies. You did not do the legwork — but you signed off every consequential turn. The output is a coherent readiness programme, and you can defend every step of it because you approved it.

How it works Templates, the vault and budget control +

The catalogue templates, including tmpl_amlr_readiness_v1, are defined in server/services/missions/seed-templates.ts. A template is a pre-decomposed task list with its checkpoints already positioned — which is why the AMLR template arrives as 10 tasks with 4 checkpoints rather than something you assemble yourself.

Credentials a Mission needs are encrypted by mission-credential-vault.ts — they are decrypted only at the moment a task uses them and are never written to logs or prompts, in line with ANTON's no-PII-in-logs rule. mission-budget.ts caps how much LLM spend a Mission may consume, and mission-checkpoint.ts enforces that flagged steps cannot proceed without a recorded human confirmation.

6Best practices

✓ Do
  • Start from a catalogue template — its checkpoints are already placed well.
  • Read the full proposal and evidence at each checkpoint before approving.
  • Give the Mission a clear goal and explicit boundaries up front.
  • Add only the credentials a Mission genuinely needs, and review what it can reach.
  • Set a budget cap so a long Mission can't run away with spend.
✕ Don't
  • Don't rubber-stamp checkpoints to keep things moving — that defeats the design.
  • Don't treat a Mission like a single prompt; it acts over many steps and over time.
  • Don't grant a credential "just in case" — reach should always be deliberate.
  • Don't ignore the inbox; a Mission stalls politely, waiting for you.
  • Don't assume an outside action is reversible — confirm before, not after.
★The Missions habit

Run Missions the way a good manager runs a delegated programme: set a clear brief, give exactly the access needed, then show up properly at the milestones. The pillar carries the effort; your attention at the checkpoints is what makes the result trustworthy — and defensible.
