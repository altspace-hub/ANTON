The Portals pillar — sites that are also endpoints

A portal is a small web space you build inside ANTON. The twist: it is two things at once. A person can visit it like any web page, and another ANTON instance can read it as a machine endpoint and invoke what it offers. Portals are ANTON's proof that instances can interoperate.

1What a portal is

Every other pillar produces work for you. Portals produce something for others: a published space that describes who you are, what you offer, and — crucially — what another ANTON can ask you to do.

The defining idea is duality. The same portal is rendered as a readable human page and exposed as a structured, machine-readable descriptor over the ANTON Agent Protocol. Build it once; it serves both audiences. That is why Portals is described as ANTON's proof of inter-instance interoperability — it is the concrete first step into Layer 4 of the vision, where ANTON instances collaborate.

⊞

A human site

A clean, readable page describing your service — anyone with ANTON can visit and browse it.

⌁

A machine endpoint

The same portal is an AAP-readable descriptor: another instance can discover and invoke its capabilities.

▤

Transparency log

Portal submissions go to a registry backed by a signed transparency log — verifiable, tamper-evident.

2How to think about a portal

🧭The mental model

Think of a portal as a shopfront with a service counter. The shopfront is the human page — the window display, the description, the reassuring detail. The service counter is the capability set — the small, precise list of things you can actually be asked to do. A good portal makes both honest: the window shows what is real, and the counter offers only what you will genuinely deliver.

Two ideas keep portal design grounded:

  • A capability is a promise. When you list a capability, you are telling other instances "ask me this and I will respond." Don't list what you can't honour — an unfulfilled invocation is worse than a missing one.
  • The descriptor is the contract. The human page is prose; the capability descriptor is the precise, structured truth a machine reads. Keep them aligned — what a visitor sees and what an instance can invoke should describe the same portal.
💡Start from a template

Portals ships with 7 starter templates. Unless your portal is genuinely unusual, begin from the closest template — it gives you a sensible structure and a starting capability set, and the 8-phase walkthrough then adapts it to you. Building from blank is rarely worth it.

3Capabilities — the 12-verb taxonomy

What a portal offers is expressed as capabilities, and every capability is built on one of 12 standard verbs. The fixed vocabulary is deliberate: a small, shared set of verbs is what lets a visiting instance understand a portal it has never seen before. The verbs cover the natural shapes of a service — answering, advising, reviewing, drafting, looking things up, accepting submissions, scheduling, and so on.

Capability shapeWhat invoking it means
Answer / adviseAsk a question; the portal owner's ANTON returns a reasoned response
Review / assessSubmit material; the portal evaluates it against a known standard
Draft / produceRequest a deliverable the portal generates from your inputs
Look up / searchQuery a body of information the portal exposes
Submit / intakeHand the portal a document or request for it to receive and process
Schedule / bookArrange a slot or commitment with the portal owner

Because the verbs are standardised, an instance discovering your portal does not need to "learn" it — it already knows what an answer capability or a review capability means. That shared taxonomy is the interoperability, made concrete.

How it works The capability descriptor and the registry protocol +

A portal's capabilities are edited via server/services/portals/portal-capabilities-editor.ts and compiled into a capability descriptor — the machine-readable contract. The portal's data model lives in migrations 145_portals_client.sql, 146_portal_content.sql, 147_portal_capability_invocations.sql and 148_portal_walkthrough_sessions.sql.

Submission and discovery go through the registry protocol under server/services/registry-protocol/: canonical-json.ts produces a deterministic canonical form so signatures are stable, envelope.ts wraps submissions in signed envelopes, and homoglyph.ts defends against look-alike-character spoofing of portal names. The registry is append-only and backed by a signed transparency log.

4Building a portal, step by step

You don't fill in a form — you are walked through it. The builder is an 8-phase walkthrough that turns a description of your service into a finished, publishable portal.

  1. Open Portals, choose a template

    Pick the closest of the 7 starter templates. It seeds the structure and an initial capability set.

  2. Describe your service

    Tell the walkthrough, in plain language, what your portal is for. ANTON uses that to enrich each phase.

  3. Work through the 8 phases

    The walkthrough covers identity, the human-facing content, the capability set, access rules and presentation — one focused phase at a time.

  4. Define the capabilities

    For each thing your portal offers, pick a verb and describe the inputs and outputs. This becomes the machine-readable descriptor.

  5. Preview both faces

    Check the rendered human page and the capability descriptor side by side — they should describe the same portal.

  6. Publish

    The portal becomes live locally and is submitted to the registry with a signed envelope; the transparency log records it.

⚠Public discovery is on a roadmap

Building, publishing, visiting and invoking portals all work today within reach of your instances. Full cross-instance public discovery — anyone, anywhere finding your portal in a public registry — is on a 6-phase roadmap, with a legal review gating the public launch. See Build status and Agent Protocol & portals for exactly where this stands.

5Worked example — a compliance clinic portal

📌Example — build → publish → visit → invoke

You run a small compliance consultancy and want a portal where peers can get quick help. You open Portals, choose the advisory starter template, and tell the walkthrough "a clinic for AML compliance questions and policy reviews." Across the 8 phases you write the human page — who you are, your specialism, your turnaround — and define two capabilities: an answer capability ("ask an AML compliance question") and a review capability ("assess an AML policy document"). You preview both faces, confirm they match, and publish. A colleague on another ANTON instance later visits your portal, reads the page, and invokes the review capability — handing your portal a draft policy. Your ANTON receives the invocation, runs the review, and returns the assessment. One build; a human read it and a machine used it.

How it works Rendering, walkthrough and invocation +

The 8-phase builder runs on portal-walkthrough-engine.ts with portal-walkthrough-templates.ts supplying the 7 starter templates; portal-llm-suggest.ts and portal-prompt-enrichment.ts turn your plain-language description into per-phase suggestions. Walkthrough progress is persisted via migration 148_portal_walkthrough_sessions.sql.

The human face is produced by portal-renderer.ts; the machine face is the capability descriptor. When another instance invokes a capability, the call is handled by portal-handler.ts and recorded in 147_portal_capability_invocations.sql. portal-search-engine.ts and portal-lan-discovery.ts cover finding portals; portal-bundler.ts packages a portal as a portable .anton bundle. The REST surface is server/routes/portals.ts.

6Best practices

✓ Do
  • Start from the closest of the 7 templates rather than a blank portal.
  • List only capabilities you will genuinely honour — an invocation is a promise.
  • Keep the human page and the capability descriptor describing the same thing.
  • Preview both faces before publishing — read it as a visitor and as a machine.
  • Use precise verbs; the 12-verb taxonomy is what lets others understand you.
✕ Don't
  • Don't advertise a capability you cannot fulfil reliably.
  • Don't let the human page over-promise what the descriptor actually offers.
  • Don't expect public, anyone-anywhere discovery yet — it is on a roadmap.
  • Don't cram many vague capabilities in; a few precise ones serve invokers better.
  • Don't skip the preview — a portal that reads well but invokes badly fails silently.
★The Portals habit

Treat your portal like a service you would be happy to be judged on. Every capability is a small, public commitment carried in a signed, tamper-evident log. A focused portal that does three things honestly is worth far more — to humans and to machines — than a sprawling one that does ten things vaguely.
